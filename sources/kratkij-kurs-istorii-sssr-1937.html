Shestakov (ed.) — Short Course of the History of the USSR

Professor A. V. Shestakov (editor) — Краткий курс истории СССР (Short Course of the History of the USSR), a school textbook for the 3rd and 4th grades, edited by Prof. A. V. Shestakov and approved by the All-Union Government Commission for the compilation of the history textbook. Uchpedgiz, Moscow, 1937. Print run 5,000,000 copies.

AuthorEdited by Prof. A. V. Shestakov, under a government commission convened to prepare a standard Soviet history textbook for the schools; approved by the All-Union Government Commission
Edition1937, Uchpedgiz (Moscow). Print run 5,000,000, first run 115,000. Colophon: 10.61 author sheets plus 9 inserts (“vkleeki”)
StructureOne volume, 250 scan pages; introduction (Введение) + fifteen chapters (I–XV), each divided into numbered sections (§1–§65), followed by a chronological table and table of contents; ~9,177 OCR lines
PeriodFrom the earliest times (the ancient Slavs, Kievan Rus) through the formation and growth of the Russian state, the imperial period, and up to the 1936 Constitution of the USSR — i.e., a full history from antiquity to the mid-1930s
NoteThe companion textbook to the Short Course of the History of the VKP(b) (1945) also in this folder; together they represent the two canonical Stalin-era textbooks of Soviet history. Approved by a government commission as the standard school history

Contents. A compressed survey of the whole of Russian and Soviet history written for the elementary grades (3–4). The introduction, “Our Motherland,” presents the basic patriotic and materialist premises of the textbook. Chapter I covers the remote past of the homeland — the life of early man, the transition from clan to state, the earliest states in the country, and the Slavs. Chapter II treats the Kievan state: the first Russian princes, Olga and Svyatoslav, the introduction of the new faith and of laws, spontaneous popular uprisings, the Novgorod land and Suzdal Rus. Chapter III deals with Eastern Europe under the Mongol conquerors — the Tatar-Mongol yoke, Moscow and the first Moscow princes, Tamerlane and the decline of the Golden Horde. Chapter IV treats the creation of the Russian national state (Ivan III and the end of the yoke); Chapter V the expansion of the Russian state (Ivan IV); Chapter VI the peasant wars and uprisings of oppressed peoples in the 17th century (Razin, the Ukraine, Siberia, culture); Chapter VII Russia in the 18th century as an empire of landlords and merchants (Peter I, Pugachev, Catherine II). Chapter VIII treats “Tsarist Russia, the gendarme of Europe” — from the French Revolution and the war of 1812 through the Decembrists, the Caucasus, 1848 with Marx and Engels, and the eve of the 1861 reform. Chapter IX covers the growth of capitalism (the abolition of serfdom, the Polish uprising, the Paris Commune, the workers’ movement of the 1870s–90s and V. I. Lenin). Chapter X is the first bourgeois revolution in Russia (1905). Chapter XI the second bourgeois revolution (the world war and the overthrow of tsarism in February 1917). Chapter XII the Great October Socialist Revolution; Chapter XIII the military intervention and civil war (Brest, the intervention, the revolutions in Western Europe, Kolchak/Denikin/Yudenich, Poland/Wrangel). Chapter XIV the transition to peaceful work of rebuilding the national economy and the creation of the USSR; Chapter XV “the USSR — the land of victorious socialism” (socialist industry and the kolkhozes, the new Constitution of 1936).

Character of the source. The Stalin-era Soviet school textbook of Russian history, prepared under a government commission and published in an enormous print run (five million copies) for the elementary schools. It presents Soviet history in the orthodox form canonized in the mid-1930s — the narrative of backwardness and oppression under the tsars, the class struggle and peasant wars, the revolutionary movement, and the triumphant building of socialism culminating in the 1936 Constitution. Written in simplified language for young readers, it nonetheless gives the complete Soviet periodisation of the national past. It is the school-level companion to the party-oriented Short Course of the History of the VKP(b) (1945) and complements the adult survey of Pokrovsky’s Russian History in the Briefest Outline (1933) also in this folder.

OCR. Good quality. The volume had no embedded text layer, so it was rendered page-by-page (ddjvu) and OCR’d with Tesseract (-l rus). 16. The strengthening of the Muscovite state. The conquest of Kazan and Astrakhan

The expansion of the Russian state in the 16th–early 17th centuries: the conquest of Kazan and Astrakhan, the reign of Ivan IV and his struggle with the boyars, and the expulsion of the Polish invaders from Moscow.

27. The wars of Peter I and popular uprisings

28. The establishment of factories and of a regular army

The 18th century: the wars and reforms of Peter I, the building of the new capital Petersburg and the creation of factories and a regular army, the peasant war of Pugachev, and the empire of the landlords and merchants under Catherine II.

VIII. Tsarist Russia — the gendarme of Europe

33. The bourgeois revolution in France and Catherine II’s and Paul I’s struggle against it

34. The war of 1812

35. The Decembrists

36. The Caucasus

37. 1848. Marx and Engels

38. Tsarist Russia — the gendarme of Europe

39. Tsarist Russia before the reform of 1861

The reactionary empire of the first half of the 19th century: the struggle of Catherine II and Paul I against the French Revolution, the war of 1812, the Decembrist uprising, the conquest of the Caucasus, the revolution of 1848 and the founders of Marxism, and the conditions in the tsarist empire on the eve of the 1861 reform.

IX. The growth of capitalism in Tsarist Russia

40. The abolition of serfdom in Russia

41. The Polish uprising of 1863

42. The First International and the Paris Commune

43. The growth of capitalism in Russia

44. The working-class movement in the 1870s–1890s. V. I. Lenin

The post-reform era: the abolition of serfdom in 1861, the Polish uprising of 1863, the First International and the Paris Commune, the growth of industrial capitalism, and the rise of the workers’ movement with V. I. Lenin.

X. The first bourgeois revolution in Russia

45. On the eve of the revolution

46. The beginning of the revolution

47. The December armed uprising

48. The defeat of the revolution

49. The new upsurge of the revolutionary movement

50. (unverified section)

The revolution of 1905: its preconditions and outbreak, the October general strike and the December armed uprising, the defeat of the revolution and the subsequent new upsurge of the revolutionary movement.

XI. The second bourgeois revolution in Russia

51. The world imperialist war

52. The overthrow of tsarism

The First World War and the defeats of Russia; the February revolution of 1917 and the overthrow of tsarism.

XII. The Great October Socialist Revolution in Russia

53. The Bolsheviks prepare the socialist revolution

54. The Great October Socialist Revolution

The preparation of the revolution by the Bolsheviks and the October Revolution of 1917, the establishment of Soviet power.

XIII. Military intervention. The civil war

55. The Brest peace. The German occupation of the Ukraine

56. The military intervention of the Entente

57. The revolution in Western Europe

58. Kolchak, Denikin, Yudenich

59. The war against Poland. The rout of Wrangel

The civil war and foreign intervention: the Brest peace and the German occupation of the Ukraine, the Entente intervention, the revolutions in Western Europe, the White generals Kolchak, Denikin and Yudenich, and the war against Poland and the rout of Wrangel.

XIV. The transition to peaceful work of rebuilding the national economy

60. The restoration of the national economy and the creation of the USSR

61. Lenin’s death. “Lenin is dead, but his cause lives on”

The transition to peaceful construction after the civil war: the restoration of the national economy, the creation of the USSR, and the death of Lenin with the slogan that his cause lived on.

XV. The USSR — the land of victorious socialism

62. Socialist industry. Collective farms (kolkhozes) in agriculture

63. The USSR — a land of socialism

64. Enemies and friends

65. The new Constitution of the USSR

The building of socialism in the 1930s: the construction of socialist industry and the collectivisation of agriculture, the USSR as a land of socialism, its enemies and friends, and the adoption of the new Constitution of 1936.
