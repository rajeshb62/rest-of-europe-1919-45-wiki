L’ère des tyrannies : études sur le socialisme et la guerre

Élie Halévy — L’ère des tyrannies: études sur le socialisme et la guerre (The Era of Tyrannies: Studies on Socialism and War). Librairie Gallimard, Paris, 1938, with a preface by Célestin Bouglé. A posthumous collection of essays and lectures by the historian of English liberalism, brought together after his sudden death in 1937, on the relation between socialism, war and the rise of dictatorial government.

AuthorÉlie Halévy (1870–1937) — French historian and philosopher, professor at the École Libre des Sciences Politiques, famed for the six-volume Histoire du peuple anglais au XIXe siècle and La Formation du radicalisme philosophique; a liberal who spent his life analysing the growth of the state and the evolution of socialism in England and Europe
Edition1938, Librairie Gallimard, Paris (copyright Gallimard 1938); printed by Imprimerie Chantenay, Paris, 21 June 1938; 65 numbered copies on alfa paper + 10 hors commerce; 257 printed pages. The text is taken from the Gallica scan of the Bibliothèque nationale de France
StructurePreface (Bouglé) + 9 essays in chronological-thematic sequence; 11,450 lines / ~744,000 characters of extracted text
PeriodThe history of socialism, the state and war from the early nineteenth century (Sismondi, Saint-Simon) through the crisis of 1914–1918, the problem of workers’ control, and the rise of the totalitarian dictatorships (Fascism, Nazism) as analysed up to Halévy’s death in 1937
NoteA philosophical-historical essay collection, not a narrative chronicle — Halévy’s object is to trace how socialism, which began as the enemy of the state, ended in alliance with a strengthened and dictatorial state, and how war and revolution together brought on “the era of tyrannies.” For this wiki it supplies the French liberal-democratic analysis of the very forces the Russian, German and Italian sources present from the revolutionary, National Socialist and Fascist sides — the diagnosis of the totalitarian state from within the democratic tradition, on the eve of the Second World War

Contents. A series of essays on the evolution of socialist doctrine and the state, from the industrial-crisis economics of Sismondi, through the Saint-Simonian school, to the great question of the twentieth century. “Sismondi” treats the Genevan economist’s critique of industrial optimism and his anticipation of the socialist analysis of crises. “La doctrine économique saint-simonienne” analyses the economic doctrine of Saint-Simon himself and then of his disciples, closing with a conclusion on their social system. “La politique de paix sociale en Angleterre” surveys the English movement of social peace and state intervention. “Le problème du contrôle ouvrier” examines the debate over workers’ control of industry, chiefly in England. “État présent de la question sociale en Angleterre” gives a present-state analysis of the English social question. “Une interprétation de la crise mondiale de 1914-1918” offers Halévy’s famous interpretation of the world crisis — towards revolution, towards war, and the mutual reinforcement of war and revolution. “Le socialisme et le problème du parlementarisme démocratique” is the 1934 lecture to the Société française de Philosophie on socialism and democratic parliamentarism. The closing essay, “L’ère des tyrannies,” the celebrated piece of 1936, argues that after the French and English revolutions the era of the new “tyrannies” — Italian Fascism, German National Socialism and Russian Bolshevism — is that of the “organised” (and in Russia the totalitarian) state.

Character of the source. A contemporary liberal diagnosis of the totalitarian danger, written across the two decades of 1914–1937. Halévy was a liberal (in the English sense) who had made his name studying the growth of the state and the history of English liberalism and socialism. The collection shows him treating socialism’s movement from hostility to the state to the use of the state, and warning that the post-1914 world, in which war and revolution had combined, had produced a new kind of organised and totalitarian tyranny that recalled the old tyrannies of the ancient world and the French Revolution. The title essay’s thesis — that Fascism and National Socialism are not reactions to the French Revolution but continuations of the “era of revolutions,” transformed by the total war and total revolution of 1914–1918 into the era of totalitarian states — makes it a key text of interwar democratic political thought.

Préface (Preface) — Célestin Bouglé

Bouglé’s preface, dated from the Société française de Philosophie, explains the genesis of the volume after Halévy’s sudden death in August 1937. His friends sought to save the unfinished part of his work — the great Histoire du Peuple Anglais had not been brought from 1848 up to 1895. Alongside that teaching at the École Libre des Sciences Politiques ran a separate course on socialism and the state, and from these studies the Société française de Philosophie had drawn the essays and lectures here collected. Bouglé recounts that at a session of the Société in the autumn of 1936, Halévy had presented a paper — “L’ère des tyrannies” — and that, in the discussion, objections had been raised: there is socialism and socialism, it was said, and against Saint-Simon stood Fourier and Proudhon, and the democracies had not yet said their last word. The objections were not resolved before Halévy’s death; in offering the texts to public discussion the editors fulfil “un pieux devoir envers la mémoire de l’ami qui nous a été enlevé” (a pious duty towards the memory of the friend who has been taken from us).

Sismondi — Critique de l’optimisme industrialiste (Sismondi: Critique of Industrialist Optimism)

A study of Jean-Charles-Léonard Simonde de Sismondi (1773–1842), the Genevan economist whom Halévy presents as “le premier annonciateur des crises” (the first announcer of crises) — the earliest critic of the optimist faith that industry and free competition would spontaneously bring general prosperity. Halévy traces Sismondi’s life and formation: born in Geneva, the old holy city of Calvin; his family exiled and ruined, the Simonde fleeing to Florence; his education and his turn to economics against Adam Smith’s Wealth of Nations. Sismondi’s Nouveaux principes d’économie politique (1819) argued that the indefinite expansion of production, without regard to consumption, would produce periodical crises and the distress of the working class, and that the remedy lay not in letting the market alone but in the deliberate action of the legislator. Halévy shows how Sismondi, who “se sent français” (feels French) despite Geneva, came to stand for a middle path between the political economy of the optimists and the nascent socialism, anticipating themes later developed by the socialist school while himself remaining a cautious reformer and friend of liberty. The essay closes with Sismondi’s death and the arrival of “de Cologne à Paris” of the manuscripts and papers that mark the transition to the socialist question proper.

La doctrine économique saint-simonienne (The Saint-Simonian Economic Doctrine)

1. — La doctrine économique de Saint-Simon (The Economic Doctrine of Saint-Simon)

The economic doctrine of Saint-Simon himself, considered as the founder of the school — his plan for the reorganization of society on the basis of industry and the “capacities,” and his conception of a new order in which the producers, the industrialists and the savants would replace the old feudal and military ruling classes.

2. — La doctrine économique des saint-simoniens (The Economic Doctrine of the Saint-Simonians)

The economic doctrine of Saint-Simon’s disciples — the school that carried his ideas into a systematic social and economic programme, the cult of industry and association, and the early socialist conceptions of property and of the state.

Conclusion

The concluding synthesis of the Saint-Simonian social and economic system — its place in the history of socialism, and its anticipations of later doctrines.

La politique de paix sociale en Angleterre (The Policy of Social Peace in England)

An essay on the English policy of “social peace” — the tradition, running through the nineteenth century, by which the English state and the English governing classes sought to reconcile the classes and to pre-empt revolutionary discontent through legislation and social reform, in contrast to the continental pattern of revolution. Halévy draws on his mastery of English history to show how England, from the factory legislation of the 1830s and 1840s through the later social reforms, sought the peaceful, gradual improvement of the condition of the working class.

Le problème du contrôle ouvrier (The Problem of Workers’ Control)

On the problem of workers’ control of industry. Halévy notes that the question “n’a pris d’importance dans les” [the problem only took on importance in the] — the text opens mid-sentence at the essay head — the debate over whether the workers should control the factories and industries in which they work, a question that gained urgency with the syndicalist and Guild-Socialist movements in England and the postwar revolutionary ferment on the continent. Halévy analyses the arguments for and against industrial democracy, and the relation of the control question to the larger socialist programme of the conquest of the state.

État présent de la question sociale en Angleterre (The Present State of the Social Question in England)

A “present-state” survey of the English social question as it stood in the interwar years — the condition of the English working class, the state of labour legislation and social policy, the decline of the old industries and the problem of unemployment, and the evolution of English socialism and trade unionism since the war.

Une interprétation de la crise mondiale de 1914-1918 (An Interpretation of the World Crisis of 1914–1918)

1. — Vers la révolution (Towards Revolution)

Halévy’s famous interpretation begins with the revolutionary dynamic of the war years — how the strain of the world war drove the European states, and above all Russia, towards revolution.

2. — Vers la guerre (Towards War)

The complementary movement towards war — how the crisis of the pre-war decades and the war itself produced the conditions in which the totalitarian movements and states could arise.

3. — Guerre et révolution (War and Revolution)

The synthesis of the essay — the mutual reinforcement of war and revolution in 1914–1918, and Halévy’s argument that the modern crisis is not a simple reaction against the French Revolution but a new and distinct phenomenon born of the combination of total war and revolution. This is the conceptual core of the collection, the thesis that underlies the closing study of the tyrannies.

Le socialisme et le problème du parlementarisme démocratique (Socialism and the Problem of Democratic Parliamentarism)

The lecture delivered to the Société française de Philosophie on socialism and the problem of democratic parliamentarism. Halévy examines the tension within socialism between its democratic, parliamentary and libertarian heritage and its revolutionary and authoritarian impulses — and how the socialist movement, in the age of the war and the dictatorships, came to face the question whether parliamentary democracy and socialism could be reconciled, or whether the one must yield to the other.

L’ère des tyrannies (The Era of Tyrannies)

The celebrated closing essay, the text of Halévy’s 1936 lecture to the Société française de Philosophie, which gives the volume its title. Halévy begins by dating the new era: “L’ère des tyrannies date du mois d’août 1914” (the era of tyrannies dates from the month of August 1914) — in other words, from the outbreak of the First World War, which by its strain and its total character set in motion the forces of total war and total revolution. He argues that the modern tyrannies — Italian Fascism, German National Socialism and, in its own fashion, Russian Bolshevism — are not simple reactions against the French Revolution and its liberal inheritance, but continuations and transformations of the revolutionary era under the new conditions of 1914–1918: the “organised” state (and in Russia the totalitarian state) mobilises the whole nation for war and revolution alike. The essay thus gives the collection its thesis — that the twentieth century’s dictatorships represent a new kind of state power, distinct both from the old absolutisms and from the liberal order of the nineteenth century, born of the convergence of war and revolution.
