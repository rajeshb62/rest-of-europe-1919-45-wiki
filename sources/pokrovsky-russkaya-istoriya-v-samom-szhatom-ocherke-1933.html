Pokrovsky — Russian History in the Briefest Outline

Mikhail Nikolaevich Pokrovsky (1868–1932) — Русская история в самом сжатом очерке (Russian History in the Briefest Outline). Fourth (combined) edition, Partizdat, Moscow, 1933. Print run 200,000 copies.

AuthorM. N. Pokrovsky — the leading Soviet Marxist historian of the 1920s; deputy people’s commissar of education of the RSFSR; head of the Institute of Red Professors; author of the standard Marxist survey of Russian history
EditionFourth, 1933 (Partizdat). The text of all three parts printed without changes from the 10th edition (Parts I–II) and the 4th edition (Part III), reviewed and corrected by the author for press in his lifetime; editor’s note added after his death (d. 10 April 1932)
StructureThree parts bound as one volume + two appendices + subject index + maps and plans; 623 scan pages; ~38,138 OCR lines
PeriodParts I–II: from the earliest times to the end of the 19th century; Part III: the revolutionary period, 1897–1917, centered on the 1905 revolution (with the introduction framing 1897–1929 and the defense of the October Revolution)
NoteVolume includes a biographical sketch of Pokrovsky and Lenin’s letter to Pokrovsky praising the work; maps by Prof. N. V. Kudryashov

Contents. The canonical Soviet Marxist survey of Russian history. Part I begins with general concepts of historical materialism (“why we need to know the past,” class struggle as the motor of history, nature and economy), then the first centuries of Russian history: the Scythians and Slavs, Kievan Rus and the “Russian Truth,” feudal Russia, the Mongol invasion, the formation of the Moscow state, Moscow’s struggle with Novgorod, the decomposition of Moscow feudalism, and the oprichnina; the “peasant revolution” of the Time of Troubles (the False Dmitrii, Bolotnikov, Minin and the fall of Moscow); the Romanov state and the schism; the Northern War and the Russian Empire. Part II (capitalism) treats the serf-owning state, commercial capitalism, the Decembrists, the populist (narodnik) revolution — Herzen, Chernyshevsky, Bakunin, Lavrov, the “Going to the People,” the People’s Will and the assassination of Alexander II — and the rise of the workers’ movement, the growth of the proletariat in the 1880s–90s, strikes, the first Marxist circles, Plekhanov and the Emancipation of Labour group. Part III covers the revolutionary period: the economy of the revolutionary era, the industrial crisis and the workers’ movement, the beginning of mass peasant movement, the Russo-Japanese War, “9 January and the Potemkin,” the peasant uprisings, the general strike and the armed uprising of October–December 1905, the “constitutional efforts” of the bourgeoisie and the Duma, the 1905 revolution in the borderlands (Caucasus, Poland, the Baltic, Finland), and the Stolypin era (“Stolypinshchina”) with its agrarian policy and state coup of 3 June 1907.

Character of the source. The definitive Soviet Marxist narrative, written by the most influential Bolshevik historian of his generation and endorsed by Lenin. Written from a strict class-struggle and historical-materialist standpoint; reflects the orthodoxy of the Soviet historical profession in the early 1930s, before the later Stalin-era attack on the “Pokrovsky school.” Part III in particular is a memoir-historian’s account of a revolution in which Pokrovsky himself participated. A companion volume in this folder — Shestakov’s Short Course of the History of the USSR (1937), a Stalin-era school textbook covering the Soviet period — has its own source page.

OCR. Good quality. This volume had no embedded text layer, so it was rendered page-by-page (ddjvu) and OCR’d with Tesseract (-l rus). Typical Cyrillic OCR errors present (e.g. “армiя” variants, Ѣ/Е confusion in pre-reform spellings, garbled Roman numerals). Headings and chapter structure checked against the table of contents.

Biographical sketch and Lenin’s letter

Introductory matter: a biographical note on Pokrovsky and the text of Lenin’s letter to Pokrovsky (from the sanitarium of the People’s Commissariat for Education, December 1920), in which Lenin asked him to write the “Russian history in the briefest outline” and warmly endorsed the projected work (“I ask you to undertake this, and we would then have a work… worthy of our attention”).

Part I. From the earliest times to the end of the 19th century

Chapter: General concepts of history

Why we need to know the past; the changeability of the world and of human life; knowledge of the past as power over the future; the “laws of history”; peasant revolutions before and now, worker revolutions; class struggle as the development of history, classes and economy, materialism; the bourgeois and proletarian understanding of history; the influence of nature — climate, seas, mountains — on culture and political development; nature and economy in Russia; the dependence of man on nature; “at the basis of history lies not nature but labour”; class struggle and the development of technique.

Chapter: The first centuries of Russian history

The Scythians; the Slavs and Slavic colonization; the life of the ancient Slavs; the accounts of foreign writers; the Russian princes, the Varangians and the Khazars; the “foundation of the Russian state”; the princes as slave-owners and slave-traders; the “Rus” and the “Russian Truth,” judicial customs, classes and class struggle reflected in it; the Novgorod commune and the “veche”; the causes of the decline of the ancient Russian cities; the “crusades”; the Tatar invasion; the transformation of Kievan Rus into a rural country; the culture of old Rus; the “Tale of Igor’s Campaign” and the chronicles; the baptism of Rus and the significance of Christianity as a class phenomenon.

Chapter: The formation of the Moscow state

Russian feudalism; the lord (boiarin) and the peasant; the forms of peasant obligations; the appanage order and the destruction of appanage fragmentation; the prerequisites of the national monarchy; why unification occurred around Moscow; the Orthodox church and its alliance with the Tatar khan and the Moscow prince; the economic ground of unification; the growth of the city of Moscow; the separation of craftsmen and merchants, the formation of the bourgeoisie; the struggle of Moscow with Novgorod (Zavoloch’e, furs and silver, European connections, the composition of the population, the outcome — Moscow as the all-Russian commercial centre); the decomposition of Moscow feudalism — commodity economy, the formation of the market, money, barter, corvée, serfdom; class struggle in the Muscovite state; the publicists, Peresvetov; the alliance of the small landowner and commercial capital; Kazan and Astrakhan; the Livonian War; the crisis of the service-land system and the coup of 1584 — the oprichnina and its class meaning; predatory economy, the flight of peasants, famine; the fugitives and the colonization of the southern borderland; the Cossacks; the growth of revolutionary mood; the False Dmitrii.

Chapter: The peasant revolution

The uprising against Godunov; Russian emigration and the Polish landlords; the Cossacks; the False Dmitrii; his victory and legislation; the conspiracy of Shuiskii; Shuiskii as the merchant-boyar tsar; the uprising of the southern Ukraine; Bolotnikov and the peasant revolution; the second Cossack revolution — Tushino; the class stratification of the revolution; the bourgeois upper layers of Tushino and commercial capital; the dethronement of Shuiskii; the alliance of the Poles with the propertied classes of Russia and its bankruptcy; the city bourgeoisie at the head of the struggle — Minin; the Nizhnii Novgorod militia takes Moscow; the candidacy of the Romanovs as the result of a new alliance of the bourgeoisie; the class meaning of the Romanov monarchy; tsarist power and commercial capital — the “gosti”; the Muscovite state and the regular army; the development of serfdom; urban uprisings of the second half of the 17th century; the schism (raskol) and the “streltsy” disorders; the Ukraine — its economic conditions, Zaporozh’e, the Cossack revolution, Khmelnytsky and the Muscovite state.

Part II. Capitalism

The serf-owning state and commercial capital; the reforms of Peter and the northern war; the Russian Empire and the Baltic question; the peasant war of Pugachev; the crisis of serfdom in the early 19th century; the growth of the bourgeoisie.

Chapter: The Decembrists

The Russian officer corps after 1812; the officer intelligentsia; the Decembrists and the bourgeoisie; the program of the Decembrists; the Union of Welfare (Soyuz Blagodenstviya); the republican current — S. Murav’ev, Pestel; the project of agrarian reform and armed uprising; the death of Alexander I and the crisis in the tsarist family; the Decembrists forced to act; their tactics and Nicholas’s tactics; why he remained victor; the Decembrists and the popular masses; the powerlessness of the bourgeois revolution in Russia and its causes.

Chapter: The populist (narodnik) revolution

Echoes of the Decembrist uprising under Nicholas: the mood of the intelligentsia; the Third Department and the gendarmes; the Petrashevtsy affair; the 1860s — the intelligentsia breaks away from the bourgeoisie; the “raznochintsy”; the political program of the petty-bourgeois intelligentsia; the “Velikoruss”; the Polish uprising; Poland under Nicholas I; the Sevastopol defeat; the Poles and Napoleon III; Alexander II’s provocative policy; the 1863 uprising and its suppression by Murav’ev; reaction in Poland; tsarist demagogy; the bourgeoisie finally breaks from revolution; petty-bourgeois socialism — its ideology; the village commune (obshchina) — its historical meaning and the hopes attached to it; Herzen; Chernyshevsky; “Young Russia”; the program of socialist revolution; Karakozovshchina and a new burst of tsarist demagogy; Lavrov, the “Historical Letters”; the philosophy and history of the petty-bourgeois intelligentsia; the practice of populism; the Chaikovskists, the Dolgushinists; “revolt” (buntarstvo), Bakunin and the Paris Commune; the “Going to the People”; its weakness — the absence of economic prerequisites for revolution; the trials; the turn in the mood of the bourgeoisie; the unfinished character of the reforms; the Turkish War; the change of tactics of the “rebels,” terror; Zhelyabov and the Executive Committee; the program of the People’s Will; Alexander II and the hunt for the “crowned beast”; the new attempt at tsarist demagogy — Loris-Melikov; 1 March 1881.

Chapter: The workers’ movement

Alexander III; the significance of the “1880s”; the economic situation — a new agrarian crisis, the revival of the “government system”; its influence on the peasantry; the “otrezki” (cut-offs), the “otrubniki”; the proletarianization of the peasantry and the unsuccessful struggle against it; the revival of serfdom — the counter-reforms of Alexander III and the role of the zemstvo; the University Statute of 1884 and the student movement; the “eighties-men” (vos’midesyatniki); the industrial proletariat and the industrial upswing of the end of the 19th century; the origin and composition of the Russian proletariat; its conditions of existence in the 1880s — housing, food, occupational diseases; its legal condition; strikes — the era of Nicholas II, the peasant reform and the proletariat; the strikes of the 1870s and the revolutionary movement; the workers’ unions; Khalturin; the industrial crisis of the beginning of the 1880s; the Morozov strike and its influence; the factory legislation of Alexander III in theory and practice; the industrial upswing of the 1890s and a new wave of strikes; Russian Marxism; Plekhanov; the Emancipation of Labour group; the first social-democratic organizations in Russia; the social-democrats and the People’s Will; economic agitation and political struggle; the unions of struggle for the emancipation of the working class.

Part III. The revolutionary period (1897–1917), centered on 1905

Introduction

“The last period of Russian history, which we still have to study (1897–1929), is entirely taken up with the preparation and immediate consequences of one great event — the Great Russian Revolution.” The introduction frames the 1905 revolution and the defense of the October Revolution of 1917, warning of the continuing threat of bourgeois intervention. In contrast to Parts I–II (which dealt with the irrevocable past), Part III explains the author’s own present.

Chapter II. The industrial crisis and the workers’ movement

Chapter III. The beginning of the mass movement in the countryside

Chapter IV. 9 January and the “Potemkin”

The shooting of the peaceful procession of workers to the Winter Palace (“Bloody Sunday,” 9 January 1905) under the leadership of Father Gapon; the mutiny of the battleship “Prince Potemkin Tauride” in June 1905, its course (the shooting of officers, the raising of the red flag, Odessa, Constanta) and its significance.

Chapter V. The Russo-Japanese War

The Japanese war of 1904–05, the defeats at Mukden and Tsushima, and their revolutionary consequences.

Chapter VI. The peasant uprisings

Chapter VII. The general strike and the armed uprising

The October general strike of 1905; the formation of the first Soviet of Workers’ Deputies in Petersburg; the December armed uprising in Moscow (Presnya) and its defeat.

Chapter VIII. The “constitutional efforts” of the bourgeoisie

The attempts of the liberal bourgeoisie to obtain a constitution; the Manifesto of 17 October 1905; the Duma; the differences between the revolution and the liberalism of the propertied classes.

Chapter IX. The 1905 revolution in the borderlands

The revolution in the Caucasus, Poland, the Baltic provinces, and Finland; the pogroms in Odessa and elsewhere; the national revolutionary movement at the edge of the empire.

Chapter X. The Stolypin era (Stolypinshchina)

Stolypin as minister of internal affairs; the state coup of 3 June 1907; the dissolution of the Second Duma and the new electoral law; the Stolypin agrarian reform and its effect on the peasantry.

Appendices

Appendix 1. On Russian feudalism, the origin and character of absolutism in Russia

Pokrovsky’s article arguing the reality of Russian feudalism and analyzing the origin and class character of Russian absolutism in connection with commercial capital.

Appendix 2. The role of the working class in the 1905 revolution

Text of Pokrovsky’s speech to the activists of the Krasnopresnensky district of Moscow (11 December 1930), comparing the 1905 revolution with the Great French Revolution and analyzing the continuing revolutionary struggle.
