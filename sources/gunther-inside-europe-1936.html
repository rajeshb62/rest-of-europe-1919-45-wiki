Inside Europe

John Gunther — Inside Europe. Harper & Brothers, New York and London; copyright 1933–1936; Revised Edition / Twenty-Fourth Edition packaging. A contemporary American journalistic survey of European personalities and politics in the mid-1930s — Hitler and the German system; Blum’s France and the Popular Front; the Spanish Civil War; Mussolini, Italy and Abyssinia; England’s ruling classes, Baldwin and Whitehall; De Valera; the Danube and Austria (February 1934, Dollfuss’s murder); Habsburg and Gömbös; Masaryk and Beneš; Lupescu’s Rumania; Yugoslavia after Alexander; Balkan kings; Atatürk; Poland without Piłsudski; the League; Stalin, his men, and “Duranty’s” Russia.

AuthorJohn Gunther (1901–1970) — American foreign correspondent and popular historian; Chicago-born, long attached to the Chicago Daily News European service; one of the best-known English-language reporters of interwar Europe, whose Inside series (Inside Europe, later Inside Asia, Inside Latin America, Inside U.S.A.) made the personal-political portrait of living leaders a mass genre

NoteExplicitly personality-driven: “the accidents of personality play a great role in history.” Gunther begins with Hitler and circles Europe around him, arguing that unresolved personal conflicts in the lives of leaders may contribute to the collapse of civilisation — an age in which millions depend for life or death on Hitler, Mussolini and Stalin. For this wiki it supplies the Anglophone contemporary panorama of Rest-of-Europe politics as seen by a working correspondent writing before 1939–45 was known

Contents. The Note states the thesis and the geography of the tour. Seven German chapters open the book: Hitler the man; the psychopathology of border-born dictators; who killed the Weimar Republic; the Reichstag fire and the blood purge of 30 June 1934; Goering and Goebbels; the lesser Nazi satraps; war, peace, foreign policy and cash after the Rhineland coup. Four French chapters treat Léon Blum, the Banque de France oligarchy and French policy, the revolving cast of Third Republic politicians, and whether fascism can take France (Stavisky, leagues, 6 February). Spain follows as civil war already underway. Three Italian chapters cover Mussolini, the Fascist hierarchy, and the Abyssinian war. Four English chapters dissect the ruling classes, Baldwin, Whitehall (MacDonald and the National Government men), and Left and Right. De Valera stands alone for Ireland. Four Austrian/Danube chapters move from Schlamperei and hopeless-but-not-serious Austria through the February Tragedy, Dollfuss’s murder, and Schuschnigg’s Austria Infelix. Then Otto Habsburg and Gömbös; Masaryk and Beneš; the Lupescu comedy; Yugoslavia after Alexander; Balkan kings; Atatürk; Poland without Piłsudski; the League at Geneva; and three Soviet chapters on Stalin, the men around him, and Gunther’s 1935 Moscow impressions under the Duranty rubric. Acknowledgments credit word-of-mouth sources across twenty capitals and list indispensable books; an Index closes the volume.

Character of the source. A mid-1930s correspondent’s Baedeker of living European power — not memoirs, not archival history, but “direct evidence” of eyes and ears checked against friends in every capital and against the contemporary press and literature. Gunther’s method is portraiture: habits, childhood, nicknames, dossiers, rivalries, and the personal sources of public power. The tone is worldly, often ironic, anti-dictatorial without being a party tract; he writes as an American liberal reporter for whom Hitler is menace and paradox, Mussolini a turncoat of genius, Blum the cultivated antithesis of the dictator type, and Stalin the most powerful single human being in the world. The book’s value for this wiki is immediacy: it freezes the European map as it looked to an informed outsider in 1935–36, with civil war in Spain begun and world war not yet certain.

Note

Gunther’s programme: the accidents of personality play a great role in history — citing Bertrand Russell on Lenin and Bismarck — while political, religious, demographic, nationalist and economic factors are not neglected; the main trend is personal. Fascism, Mussolini’s creation, has produced its first war; unresolved personal conflicts in the lives of politicians may contribute to the collapse of civilisation. This is the age of great dictatorial leaders — Hitler, Mussolini, Stalin — and of lesser careerists (Laval, Goering, Gil Robles, Gömbös, Rydz-Śmigły, Zog, Carol). The book is “not a peephole book”; it tries to tell the intimate story of these leaders and the personal sources of their power. It begins with Hitler and tours counter-clockwise: France, Spain, Italy; detour to England; Central Europe and the Balkans; Poland; Geneva; the Soviet Union.

Chapter I — Hitler

Hitler is irrational, contradictory, complex and unpredictable — therein lie his power and his menace: adored by millions of Germans, to others a charlatan and lying demagogue. Born in Austria in 1889, not a German by birth, he developed the implacable patriotism of the frontiersman and exile. Gunther opens with character and daily routine before biography: purely political imagination, prosaic early paintings rejected by the Vienna academy, little culture, no foreign travel of note, no foreign language, ranting rather than conversation. Friends are scarce — Roehm was the only man on Duzfuss terms; after June 1934 almost none remain intimate. The portrait covers habits, Berchtesgaden, the Wilhelmstrasse Chancellery, reading and oratory, and the sources of mesmeric hold over the German public — the human being who is also party chief, commander-in-chief and creator of the Third Reich.

Chapter II — Psychopathology of Dictators

Not only Hitler but several modern leaders were born in border zones or foreign countries: Atatürk in Salonika, Piłsudski of Lithuanian origin, Schuschnigg in Italy, Stalin a Georgian. Gunther visits Braunau — the birth house a three-storey Gasthaus, not a rustic log cabin — and the Waldviertel kin country: cousins, godfather, midwife, the inbred peasant stock. Family tree, childhood legends of starvation (dismissed), and the psychological pattern of the border-born dictator who takes nationalism more seriously than natives. The chapter frames Hitler’s early life as case-study in the pathology and mythology of modern personal power.

Chapter III — Who Killed the German Republic?

Personal qualities alone would not have brought Hitler power; they helped him identify himself with a large percentage of the German nation — demigod because he persuaded Germans that he alone represented renascent Germanism. Germany was late to nationhood; Nazi apologists call the terror and blunders of foreign policy the pangs of adolescence. Defeat in the War, the “stab in the back” legend against Jews, socialists and pacifists, and the false deduction that Germany had not really lost — Hitler’s technique was to tell Germans they were sick and that he alone could cure them. The chapter assigns responsibility for Weimar’s death among republicans, industrialists, army, parties and the demagogue who harvested humiliation.

Chapter IV — The Trick by Fire and the Purge by Blood

On the night of 27 February 1933 the Reichstag burned — destroying not only a building but what remained of the German republic, incinerating communist, social-democratic, Catholic and nationalist parties. The fire produced the March 5 electoral victory, Brown Terror, persecution of Jews, offensive against Austria and Gleichschaltung. Gunther narrates the evening’s chronology (Goering and Hitler on the scene blaming communists) and, with Douglas Reed, the end of the rule of law. The second half turns to 30 June 1934: the blood purge that killed Roehm, Schleicher, Strasser and others — “Al Capone” methods inside Germany — amid SA discontent, fear of a “second revolution,” and Hitler’s justification by plot. Fire and purge together mark the birth and consolidation of the Third Reich.

Chapter V — The Two G-Men

Behind Hitler stands a fantastic congeries of sub-Hitlers — cynics, hysterics, men on-the-make; yet many decent Germans also serve. The chapter’s centre is the pairing of Goebbels (crippled envy as propaganda chief of “popular enlightenment”) and Goering (violent, unstable, ex-morphiniac with near-supreme police power). Hitler plays subordinates against one another; when Goering grew too powerful the secret police went to Himmler. Party posts imperfectly match state posts — Schacht has no party rank; Rosenberg is party foreign chief without being foreign minister. The two G-men embody the Nazi system’s personal rivalry and institutional confusion.

Chapter VI — The Other Little Hitlers

After Goering and Goebbels, Wilhelm Frick — precise Beamter, minister of the interior, “perfect bureaucrat,” drafter of Aryan laws, once “inside man” for the 1923 putsch — is the most powerful officeholder. Around him swirl Rust (education), Rosenberg, Darré, Hess, Himmler, Streicher and the lesser satraps competing for culture, police and ideology. Gunther sketches the intra-party battle over art and Weltanschauung and the gallery of secondary Hitlers who administer the Gleichgeschaltet Reich.

Chapter VII — War, Peace, Policy, and Cash

On 7 March 1936 Hitler remilitarised the Rhineland, tearing up Locarno and splitting Europe “like a rotten melon.” French hesitation, British temporising, Italian refusal to join a Locarno front after sanctions, and Belgian alarm under Van Zeeland fill the diplomatic aftermath; Ribbentrop accepts an academic League rebuke. The chapter widens to Nazi foreign policy, rearmament, Schacht’s economic dictatorship, and the cash-and-credit machinery of the Third Reich — war, peace and money as one system after the coup that tested whether anyone would stop Hitler.

Chapter VIII — Léon Blum

Léon Blum, socialist “exquisite” of the Île Saint-Louis, is Gunther’s key counterweight in the world struggle between Fascism and democracy — man of thought against men of action, silver poniard of wit against the bruiser’s fist. Leader of the Popular Front (idea that matured in Austria, Saar, Spain, then France), he is not a man of the masses; ambassadors note he is not primarily a “politician”; with Eden he talks Proust; in London he slips into the British Museum to see the Elgin marbles. Portrait of the cultivated individualist who emerged from the ivory tower to confront Hitler and Mussolini as Président du Conseil of the Front Populaire.

Chapter IX — French Policy—and Why

Until the Popular Front’s provisional victory the French prime minister was a creature of the financial oligarchy — the celebrated Deux Cents families whose pediment was the Banque de France. Private bank issuing public money; only the top two hundred shareholders vote for the regents; Coty and Citroën never entered the velvet circle. Blum may fall; the Bank and the “200” go on. Gunther traces how gold, credit and the hereditary bourgeoisie shaped French foreign and domestic policy — why France acts as it does when gold’s friends must be wise or face revolution (Keynes) and when the proverb says the only way to treat a Prussian is to step on his toes until he apologises.

Chapter X — More About Frenchmen

Germany is Hitler; France is a whole lot of people — 618 deputies, ninety-seven cabinets in sixty-four years, fourteen living ex-prime ministers. Extreme fluidity: heart on the Left, pocketbook on the Right (Siegfried); parties mistitled and overlapping; coalitions and individualism. Foreign observers exaggerate the whirligig — the permanent civil service and deeper continuities remain. Portraits of Herriot, Laval, Tardieu, Daladier, Caillaux and the revolving cast who make Third Republic politics a reductio of democracy and still a functioning machine.

Chapter XI — Fascism in France?

Inner history of the Stavisky case: petty gangster with “pull,” provisional freedom nineteen times postponed, brother-in-law of Chautemps at the Parquet; Right leagues, Action Française, Comité des Forges seize the scandal to wreck Freemasons, radicals and the Left. From Stavisky to 6 February 1934 and the question whether France can go Fascist — Croix de Feu, street leagues, and Gunther’s assessment that French individualism and republican habit still obstruct a full Hitlerite capture, even as the oligarchy and the leagues probe for openings.

Chapter XII — The Spanish Civil War

On 18 July 1936 civil war breaks out; rebels hold Morocco, Gibraltar region and much of the north; government holds Madrid, Málaga, Barcelona; Italian planes in Morocco raise intervention fears. Gunther refuses the conservative press’s “loyalist/Nationalist” label for Franco and Mola’s rising — it is a Fascist revolt against a liberal democratic regime with socialist and communist support (unlike Blum’s France, Spanish socialists stood aloof from cabinet responsibility). Flashback through Azaña’s rise, the Republic, Lerroux, Gil Robles, and the Castillo–Calvo Sotelo murders as twin flames; army mostly rebel, navy mostly government, people’s militia holding half of Spain in patches — Left versus Right, “and the Right was in the wrong.”

Chapter XIII — Mussolini

Benito Mussolini — tempestuous, ornate, blacksmith’s son, creator of modern Italy and author of the Abyssinian war — born 1883 at Dovia di Predappio. Career of the most formidable combination of turncoat, ruffian and man of genius in modern history; few accidental “sealed train” moments — he made his own luck. Early influence of Angelica Balabanova in Switzerland; Lenin’s later rebuke that Italian socialists had “lost” their best man. Voracious reader and writer, best educated of the dictators, architect of Fascism as doctrine and spectacle; Gunther’s full personality-and-power portrait from Romagna socialism through March on Rome to the Duce who would make his life a masterpiece.

Chapter XIV — Who Else in Italy?

Mussolini is three things: Duce of the party, Capo del Governo, head of the Grand Fascist Council — posts distinct yet merged in his person; unlike Hitler he keeps party and state theoretically separate, like Stalin, but the Council are his underlings. He alone among dictators has arranged succession via the Grand Council’s secret list for the King. No Goering, no Kaganovich: when Grandi or Balbo grow prominent they are removed; Starace survives by subservience. Can the system survive the Duce? Gunther inventories the quadrumvirs, hierarchy and small fish around the man who is the Fascist system.

Chapter XV — War in Abyssinia

October 1935: Mussolini sets out “to rob and conquer a country he had promised to defend.” Why 1935, why Abyssinia? Italy’s fundamentals — forty-two million on one-third France’s arable land, 450,000 extra mouths a year, scarce raw materials, exposed coastline — drive the hunger for colonies. Sanctions, League failure, and the cost of transforming a poor country into a great power frame the campaign; Fascism’s first war becomes the test that pushes Rome toward Berlin and exposes Geneva’s weakness.

Chapter XVI — England: The Ruling Classes

England is at once firmest monarchy, strongest oligarchy and freest democracy — an island of country houses on coal. Two per cent of property owners hold sixty-four per cent of national wealth; a fluid impregnable caste lives by land and trade, conscience and clubs, Commons and Lords, navy, Bank of England and civil service. Gunther maps land ownership in London, income shares, company directors in the Commons, and the manners of a ruling class that believes in free speech yet absorbs a disproportionate share of power — the social pedestal of Baldwin’s England.

Chapter XVII — Baldwin

Stanley Baldwin seriously damaged his reputation in the Hoare–Laval crisis — repudiating a foreign policy and then a foreign secretary — yet remains the most powerful contemporary European democrat. Obscure fifteen years earlier, now personification of John Bull: solid, sober, unexcited, “spiritual home… the last ditch,” also a sort of Scandinavian mystic who gropes rather than thinks. Character over circumstance; the amateur who is a superb professional (Laski); dictatorship as a beech under which nothing grows — Baldwin’s own warning as democratic foil to the dictators.

Chapter XVIII — Men of Whitehall

Opens with the débâcle of Ramsay MacDonald — “Judas Iscariot” to Labour, creator and destroyer of the party — illegitimate poverty, War pacifism, Chance’s double gift of office, National Government and the duchesses’ kiss. Around him the Whitehall gallery: Simon, Hoare, Eden, Vansittart, the permanent officials and National ministers who run British foreign policy between Locarno’s ruin and Abyssinia’s shame. Men of the machine rather than of the mass meeting.

Chapter XIX — Left and Right in England

Socialism in England is not revolutionary — not even depressed areas, two million unemployed and Stafford Cripps make it so; the smallest white-collar clerk thinks himself the opposite of a workman (Mirsky). Fabian “inevitability of gradualness,” weak Marxism, Labour’s muddled choices in office (Dalton’s three alternatives none taken). Trade unions, I.L.P., exiguous communists, and the Conservative Right — Beaverbrook, diehards, Empire — map a country where Left and Right quarrel within a shared parliamentary civilisation.

Chapter XX — De Valera

Like Hitler, Piłsudski, Stalin, Atatürk and Schuschnigg, Eamon de Valera was not born a citizen of the country he rules — New York, 1882, Spanish-Cuban father, Irish mother; American birth saved him from execution after Easter 1916 when every other commandant was shot. Universally “Dev,” a nickname of intimacy rare among modern chieftains. Gunther’s portrait of the austere mathematician-revolutionary who built the Free State’s opposite pole to Cosgrave, and whose American accident may have made Irish independence itself possible.

Chapter XXI — Danube Blues

“The situation in Germany is serious but not hopeless; the situation in Austria is hopeless but not serious” — Viennese saying. Adler’s Absolutismus gemildert durch Schlamperei still holds, yet 1934 taught that Viennese could take to the gun. Tiny post-imperial Austria (size of Maine, population under New York City) adjusting from fifty-million empire to exiguity; Föhn, coffee, Mozart — and the Balkans beginning at the Landstrasse. Scene-setting for the February civil war and the Nazi assault on Dollfuss: charm under sentence of geopolitics, Hitler’s homeland next door.

Chapter XXII — The February Tragedy

The civil war of February 1934 was called a socialist “revolution” by Dollfuss; Gunther insists it was a cold-blooded Heimwehr Fascist coup — socialists resisted as Belgians resisted in 1914. Bauer and Deutsch were not Bolsheviks; they had saved Austria from Bolshevism in 1919; Schutzbund arms had once been shared with the army. Heimwehr under Starhemberg and Fey had threatened extirpation since 1927; Fey at a parade: “To-morrow we are going to clean up Austria.” Chronology from Tyrol demands to 12 February fighting in Linz and Vienna — artillery against workers’ flats, Gemütlichkeit gone wrong, social democracy smashed as prelude to the clerical-authoritarian state.

Chapter XXIII — Death of Dollfuss

The murder of Dollfuss (25 July 1934) marked gangsterism’s entrance into European politics on an international basis — June 30 methods crossing the border. Organised SS conspiracy: 89th SS regiment on Austrian soil, dismissed Nazi army men, tainted police; support from Habicht and Frauenfeld in Germany, Vienna police executives, and “King Anton” Rintelen in Rome as respectable transitional chancellor — Austria’s Papen. Gunther reconstructs the Chancellery raid, the dying Dollfuss denied a priest, the failed Putsch, and Mussolini’s mobilisation on the Brenner — assassination as deliberate Nazi policy when every other method failed.

Chapter XXIV — Austria Infelix

Kurt von Schuschnigg — born 1897 on Lake Garda when it was Austrian, Italian prisoner in the War, now prisoner of the Italians if the Germans don’t get him next week — holds the chancellorship precariously. Cold, severe, honest, undemagogic Catholic-monarchist protégé of Seipel; founder of the Sturmscharen as Tyrolean offset to Hitlerism and to Starhemberg’s Heimwehr. Dull enough that Viennese joke no joke about him will ever toll St Stephen’s bells — yet the dutiful heir of Dollfuss’s authoritarian Austria, squeezed between Rome and Berlin, infelix in the interval before Anschluss.

Chapter XXV — Dr. Otto Habsburg and Gen. Julius Goemboes

Seven hundred years of Habsburg madness, murder and melancholia stand behind young Otto — pretender with a litany of Christian names, fertile Bourbon-Parma blood, heir to realms that were always family before state. Restoration politics in Vienna and Budapest intertwine with Admiral Horthy’s Hungary and General Julius Gömbös — careerist on-the-make, authoritarian nationalist, Gunther’s type of the Danubian adventurer. Monarchy as ghost and Gömbös as living strongman: the Habsburg organism still attached to Central Europe’s body politic.

Chapter XXVI — Masaryk and Benes

Masaryk — son of a serf who created a nation; blacksmith boy; pacifist who organised the Czechoslovak Siberian legion; philosopher-statesman; firm democrat who still believes in rule by tolerance; the man who more than any other smashed old Austria-Hungary so that Czechoslovakia rose as stablest of the succession states. Paradoxes of autobiography: coachman’s son in the imperial castle; conspirator whose basis was moral-intellectual. Beside him Beneš, the foreign-minister architect of alliances and League policy — Bohemia as Bismarck’s “master of Europe,” a small nation proving immense value with limited means.

Chapter XXVII — The Lupescu Comedy

Rumania — oil, grain, timber, corrupt politicians; Bucharest a tinselly little Paris; Bratianu semi-dictators and baksheesh as national watchword; agrarian debt highest per capita in the world. Cruel joke: “Rumania” as kleptomania applied to a nation. King Carol ruled in turn by Magda Lupescu — the comedy of mistress, dynasty (Marie of Edinburgh’s line), Iron Guard undercurrents and Balkan royal soap opera as serious politics. Gunther’s light title covers a dark structure of exploitation and personal rule.

Chapter XXVIII — Jugoslavia After Alexander

Peter II, twelve years old, shy, linguistically hesitant, mismanaged education under an English Nanny — “I am too young to be a king” — titular ruler of 13.5 million Serbs, Croats, Slovenes and others after Alexander’s Marseille assassination. Contrast with Carol’s sensible schooling of Michael of Rumania. Regency, Stojadinović, Croat–Serb tension, and the murdered king’s centralising legacy: Yugoslavia as powder magazine held by a boy and the men who speak for him.

Chapter XXIX — Balkan Kings

Gallery of sub-monarchs — above all Zog of Albania (“the Bird”), Moslem chieftain of the Mati, blood feuds by the hundred, legends of kidnapped brides and guerrilla prowess, king since 1928 of Europe’s smallest country. Other Balkan crowns (Bulgaria, Greece, the royal Balkan Entente) sketched as anachronistic personal regimes in Jerome Frank’s teasing question: must every little language have a country all its own? Personality politics at the southeastern margin of Gunther’s circle around Germany.

Chapter XXX — The Turkish Colossus

The blond, blue-eyed patriot-psychopath who rules Turkey has changed his name seven times — from Mustafa to Kemal to Ghazi to Atatürk to Kamal Atatürk — and westernised the alphabet so that Constantinople streets read like phonetic comedy (Foks Film Korporeysen, Lozan Palas Otel). Gunther’s portrait of the destroyer of caliphate and Greeks, Father of Turks, moderniser by decree — colossus on the edge of Europe whose personal will remade language, law and capital as thoroughly as any western dictator.

Chapter XXXI — Poland Without Pilsudski

Nationalism plus personality created Poland; Piłsudski died in 1935 but the state remains his instrument. From the Kadrówka of three hundred men in August 1914 to Magdeburg prison to head of state — the Legionnaires still rule: Kasprzycki, Sosnkowski, Rydz-Śmigły and the clique of the marshal’s officers. Poland as Europe’s great unpredictable, a régime of living idiosyncrasy after the gruff old walrus is gone — proof of Gunther’s thesis that nationalism is best represented politically by the power of personality.

Chapter XXXII — Half a League Onward

Easy to sneer at the League — Wilson’s virgin in a bawdy house, America’s only spoils of Versailles, stable doors after horses have fled, secret settlements, feeble committees. Yet the states, not Geneva, bear responsibility; the League is no super-state. American refusal to ratify torpedoed sanctions; Abyssinia exposed the rest. Gunther’s Geneva chapter inspects Western Europe’s collective security machine at the moment of its clearest failure — half a league onward, into futility.

Chapter XXXIII — Stalin

Stalin is the most powerful single human being in the world — and of the second generation of dictators, taking over from Lenin against Lenin’s explicit wish (“too rude… remove him”). Extolled as Beloved, Genius, Guiding Star; cities named for him. Sources of power: guts, basalt calm (no Hitler neurotics, no Mussolini emotion), inhuman patience, brick-laying tenacity (Duranty). Georgian cobbler’s son, seminary boy, underground name “steel”; party boss who refuses to coalesce into ceremonial head of state. Gunther’s long portrait of the man who leads both a national state and the Communist International — leadership as fight on two fronts against those who lag and those who rush ahead.

Chapter XXXIV — Men Around Stalin

Unlike Nazi morphiniacs and hysterics, the men around Stalin — Kaganovich, Molotov, Ordzhonikidze, Chubar, Mikoyan, Voroshilov — are able, disinterested servants of the state without genius for publicity. Of ten Politburo members, four never went to school; none has a university education — hence red tape, yet a diplomat’s judgment that they are as able as any governing body in the world. Pattern: manual workers turned underground revolutionaries; party entry date as hierarchy; prison as badge of new nobility; fanatics of a material Jesuitry. The anonymous collective that rules one-sixth of the globe.

Chapter XXXV — “Duranty’s Inferno”

What one most objects to in the Soviet Union is often Russian, not communist — cruelty, slovenliness, espionage, dirt, inefficiency (Baedeker 1914 already required exit visas). Gunther’s 1935 return after seven years: visa bother and dead telephones as pin-pricks; Moscow still the most refreshing city in Europe against the neurotic masochism of the West. Rising living standard (still miserably low), order and direction versus German hysteria and Spanish or Polish muddle. Closes with Soviet foreign policy — Laval and Beneš treaties as ring of steel around Germany, Stalin’s order that French communists support the French army, the 1935 Comintern congress as sop — Russia as Duranty’s contested “inferno” that Gunther finds, comparatively, awake.

Acknowledgments and Bibliography

About two-thirds of the book, Gunther estimates, comes from direct evidence of his own eyes and ears; the rest from scores of unnamed friends in twenty countries (many under dictatorships) plus careful manuscript checking. No formal academic bibliography, but an indispensable list: Heiden on National Socialism, Hitler’s My Struggle, Armstrong, Bauer, Beneš, Capek/Masaryk, Chamberlin, Duranty, Finer on Mussolini’s Italy, Gedye’s Heirs to the Habsburgs, Knickerbocker, Ludwig, Trotsky, and dozens more — the working library of a 1930s foreign correspondent.
