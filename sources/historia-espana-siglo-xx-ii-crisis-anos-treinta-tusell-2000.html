Full bibliographic citation — Javier Tusell Gómez (1945–2005). Historia de España en el siglo XX, volume II, La crisis de los años treinta: República y guerra civil. — First published 2000. This text from the ePubLibre digital edition (ePUB r1.3, JeSsE, 6 February 2014; UUID e6d70ae1-5e32-42f8-bb91-60437555f4ef; hash 8e15882a1410ffd7a2485eb09611e5c3). The four-volume series covers the Spanish twentieth century; volume II studies the crisis of the decisive thirties, in which the Second Republic and the Civil War marked the lives of Spaniards. It is the Republic-and-Civil-War companion to volume III (La dictadura de Franco), already held in this wiki.

Javier Tusell, catedrático of Contemporary History at the UNED and a leading Spanish political historian of the twentieth century, wrote the volume as a full-scale scholarly synthesis of the Republic and the Civil War — not a memoir or a party history, but a modern secondary survey. It opens on society and economy before politics: the agrarian and urban world, daily life, the impact of the world economic crisis, and the coming of mass democracy. It then traces the democratic experience of the Second Republic from the 1931 elections through the Constitution, the reformist biennium (military, religious, agrarian and social reforms), the «República en una tenaza» of monarchists and anarchists, the conservative biennium of radicals and cedistas, the insurrection of October 1934 and its aftermath, the split and radicalisation of the socialists, Spanish fascism, the Popular Front of 1936, and the “plano inclinado” toward the Civil War, before turning to the war itself — conspiracy, balance of forces, revolution and repression, the church, the column-war, Madrid, internationalisation, the northern campaign, the formation of the two armies, Franco’s political unity, the Popular Front’s internal divisions, Negrín’s government, Teruel and the march to the Mediterranean, the Ebro, and the end of the war — closing with society and culture in wartime and a long bibliography.

Part I — Sociedad y economía

Plenitud y crisis de los años treinta

Tusell’s framing chapter on why the thirties carry an almost impossible-to-exaggerate weight in Spanish contemporary history: the density of the period, the sense that its events refuse to be narrated like any other era, and the claim that Spain’s twentieth-century peculiarity derives from these years. The democratic experience of 1931 onward was not an ordinary phenomenon, however comparable to parts of Eastern Europe in the first postwar, because the Second Republic’s reforming social and participatory political purposes went far deeper than any such parallel.

Medio rural y urbano

The agrarian and urban social world of 1930s Spain — the latifundist and smallholding countryside, the growing cities and their class structure — as the setting for the political conflicts to come.

El ritmo de la vida cotidiana

Everyday life in the thirties: work, wages, prices, leisure, housing and the material texture of daily existence in a society still largely poor and unequally distributed.

El impacto de la crisis económica

The impact of the world economic crisis of the early 1930s on Spain — slower to arrive than elsewhere but with grave social consequences — and its role in sharpening the agrarian and labour problems the Republic inherited.

La democracia de masas: Movilización política y experiencia de la democracia

Essential to the republican years was a decisive change in the way politics was lived: the Republic was not merely a change of regime but the advent of the masses to public life, which Primo de Rivera’s dictatorship had hidden and then made abrupt. Tusell insists the Second Republic was the Spanish democratic experience par excellence before 1977 — never before (under oligarchic liberalism) nor after had Spain mass participation in public life on the pattern of the democracies. This affirms a fact, not a value-judgment, and does not presume the republican leaders always chose well.

Part II — La República

La experiencia democrática republicana

The founding democratic experience of 1931: the elections, the fall of the monarchy, the provisional government and the hopes and divisions of the opening of the regime.

La elaboración de la Constitución

The drafting of the 1931 Constitution in the constituent Cortes — its separation of church and state, territorial and social provisions, and the political alignments that produced it.

La reforma militar

The reformist biennium’s military reform under Azaña — the prestige his ministerial work earned him, and the restructuring of an over-large officer corps and army. The religious question, dividing the provisional government in the Cortes, imposed a new governing formula and opened what Tusell calls the reformist biennium, directed by Azaña.

La cuestión religiosa

The religious question as the sharpest anticlerical-clerical cleavage of the republican years — very acute in the first biennium, less so later — and the Church’s place in a society where, whatever the cultural and educated middle classes had become, the whole of Spain could not be said to have ceased being Catholic.

La reforma agraria y la agitación social en el campo

The agrarian reform as the most ambitious problem facing the new regime, appearing before the constitution was drafted and, like the religious question, decisive in alienating a large part of the population. Even with the timidity of its content and application it confronted a multisecular problem made urgent by the economic crisis, social expectations and a growing population the labour market could not absorb; the accompanying social agitation in the countryside.

La pluralidad española

The regional and linguistic pluralism of Spain — Catalan and Basque questions in particular — as a defining problem of the Republic and its territorial organisation.

La política económica y las reformas sociales

The Republic’s economic policy and social reforms: legislation on labour, wages, social insurance and welfare, and their limits in the crisis conditions of the early thirties.

La «República en una tenaza»: monárquicos y anarquistas

The Republic “in a pincer” between monarchists on the right and anarchists on the left — the two anti-republican extremes that, from opposite directions, pressured and destabilised the democratic experiment.

La crisis del bienio reformista y las elecciones de 1933

The crisis of the reformist biennium and the 1933 elections that ended it — the disintegration of the centre-left coalition and the shift of power that brought the conservative biennium.

Radicales y cedistas: el comienzo de la colaboración

The beginning of the collaboration between the Radical Party of Lerroux and the CEDA (Confederación Española de Derechas Autónomas) that governed the second, centre-right biennium.

La insurrección de octubre de 1934

The October 1934 insurrection — the socialist and Catalan revolt against the entry of the CEDA into government, opened here through the figure of Ricardo Samper who briefly headed the caretaker cabinet and was respected by neither right nor left. The PSOE’s sense of abandonment after 1933 as background to the rising; Azaña’s preference for «el rey y sus ministros» against the rebels.

Las secuelas de octubre y la agonía inacabable del segundo bienio

The aftermath of October and the “interminable agony” of the second biennium — repression, the suspension of Catalan autonomy, and the slow disintegration of the radical-cedista coalition through 1935.

División y radicalización de los socialistas españoles

The split within Spanish socialism — reformists versus revolutionaries (Prieto against Largo Caballero) — and its radicalisation that pushed the party toward revolution, a course with consequences for the Republic’s stability.

Fascismo en España

The curious parallel between far-right and far-left: direct communist and fascist influence in Spain was quite small until the outbreak of the Civil War, even though totalitarian styles seemed to pervade political life. The fascist extreme, divided and nearly without parliamentary representation, was a negligible social and political force at the start of 1936 — even more marginal than the communists — its one success being in university student circles.

El Frente Popular y las elecciones de 1936

The Popular Front and the February 1936 elections: the recomposition of the left (Azaña’s republican-left position volatilised by 1933 in seats though less in votes) and the election that returned the Popular Front to government.

El Frente Popular en el Gobierno

The Popular Front in government: the programme and personnel of the new cabinet, its early measures and the quickening of social and political conflict under it.

El plano inclinado hacia la Guerra Civil

The “inclined plane” toward the Civil War — the spring and summer of 1936, the accelerating polarisation, violence and political paralysis that brought the Republic to the point of the military rising, with the argument that until nearly the last moment the war could have been avoided.

Política exterior española en los años treinta

Spanish foreign policy in the thirties: the Republic’s place in the Europe of the League of Nations and the dictatorships, and the neutrality and relations that framed the approach of 1936.

Plenitud y crisis en los medios culturales

Spanish culture in the republican years as a period of “plenitude and crisis”: the twenties vanguard had reached only small minorities, but the thirties produced a project of cultural modernisation rooted in Madrid and Barcelona and diffused to urban and partly rural media — the Republic as, in many senses, the beginning of the tradition of modernity in Spanish cultural history (urbanism, quality theatre in the capital, the diffusion of the plastic-arts vanguard, supporting legislation).

Part III — La Guerra Civil

La Guerra Civil española

Tusell’s framing of the war as one of only two moments when Spain played a protagonist role in twentieth-century world history (the other being the Transition), and a warning against the interpretive axis that makes all Spanish contemporary history an inevitable road to the conflict between two death-gripped sectors of society. The war was not inevitable; it could have been avoided until nearly the last moment. Eyewitnesses, especially those with major political responsibility, tended to deny this, perhaps to exculpate themselves, but they refer only to the final stretch — the proof that it could have been avoided lying in how different behaviour by Casares Quiroga, or his earlier replacement, might have averted it.

La conspiración contra la República

The conspiracy against the Republic: although the war’s outbreak cannot be attributed to external factors despite Italy’s aid to monarchists, traditionalists and falangists — that help neither sufficed to finance the rising nor grew at the moment of it — the extreme-right groups had sought Italian fascist help. The republican authorities published the monarchists’ 1934 pacts with Mussolini to show an early conspiracy, but Italian aid was only decisive from July 1936 onward.

Un primer balance de fuerzas: España dividida en dos

A first balance of forces at the outset of the war — Spain divided in two: the geographic, military and material resources of each side in July 1936 and the initial prospects of the conflict.

La revolución y sus consecuencias

The revolution and its consequences, opened with Azaña’s retrospective verdict that after the rising the republican government faced both the military movement advancing on Madrid and the insurrection of the proletarian masses who, without directly attacking the government, did not obey it — who wanted to make a syndical revolution to combat fascism — and that the government’s chief task through the whole war was to “reduce those masses to discipline.” The social revolution in the republican zone and its consequences for the war’s conduct.

La represión en la retaguardia

An immediate consequence of the war’s becoming irreversible was that individuals on both sides demonised the adversary and judged it most urgent to exterminate him physically. Initial tolerance or pacts of neutrality soon gave way; the repression was both the testimony that the Civil War had begun and a contributor to making it irreversible — once the blood flowed, an abyss opened between the belligerents that could not close. Terror, not social revolution or the restoration of traditional principles, came first on both sides.

El catolicismo ante la Guerra Civil

Catholicism before the Civil War: the religious factor’s enormous weight in social and political life. Against Azaña’s claim that Spain had ceased to be Catholic, Tusell finds arguments both ways — the cultural world and much of the educated middle classes had ceased to be, but the claim cannot extend to the whole society, and the very sharpness of the clerical-anticlerical contrast shows the question was not indifferent to Spaniards.

La guerra de columnas

The first military phase as a war of columns: successive clashes between not-very-large groups without a precise front, in which disparity of numbers and quality made one side almost always defensive; the heterogeneous composition of these armed nuclei mixing the military and the revolutionary.

La batalla en torno a Madrid. La guerra en el mar

The failure of Franco’s direct assault on Madrid in November 1936 as a decisive military turn — the column-war exhausted as fronts consolidated and the popular militias grew more efficient, at least on the defensive. Franco’s coherent response: abandoning the direct assault for a flanking manoeuvre that would draw the adversary onto open ground where his superiority had been shown. The naval war and the blockade that accompanied the fight around Madrid.

La internacionalización de la Guerra Civil

The internationalisation of the war: though internal in origin, the war made Spain the “centre of the world’s passions and disappointments.” Without foreign aid the crossing of the Strait, the defence of Madrid, Guadalajara and Franco’s maritime hegemony cannot be understood; both contenders sought help because the Spanish army was badly equipped materially. The international conjuncture of the moment was already tense.

Las potencias y la guerra española

The powers and the Spanish war: the stance and interest of the major European (and some American) states from the conflict’s outset — the Non-Intervention Committee and its limits, and the balance of aid to each side.

La campaña del norte

Franco’s decision, despite initial resistance, to move the war’s centre of gravity to the northern zone at the end of March 1937 — a sound decision involving a “detour” to his final objectives but yielding, at the conflict’s “critical point,” a victory decisive for its end. The war was resolved in the northern campaign: from April to October the rebel army successively conquered Vizcaya, Santander and Asturias, transforming the initial balance of July 1936.

Aspectos económicos de la guerra

Economic aspects of the war: finance, production, blockade, scarcity and the economic management of both zones during the conflict.

La formación de dos Ejércitos y la conducción de la guerra

The formation of the two armies — the Popular Army in the republican zone and the regular command structure in the rebel zone — and the conduct of the war as each side’s forces professionalised.

Unidad política en torno a Franco

Political unity around Franco: like the Popular Front, the first result of the rising in the rebel zone was the fragmentation of political authority — but here, without bloodshed, a high degree of unity was achieved over time, explained by the peculiar mentality of the rebels, who sought above all to prevent the triumph of a revolution they felt as imminent though neither prepared nor with a capable political group. A preventive counter-revolution whose result was a revolution of disparate contents.

El enfrentamiento en el seno del Frente Popular

The confrontation within the Popular Front: the political crisis of the republican zone in 1937 — the conflict between the anarchist and communist-influenced currents, Largo Caballero’s government and its fall, and the growing communist role.

El gobierno Negrín

The Negrín government: Azaña and Prieto as the true authors of the political resolution, not any of the named groups. Azaña had deplored Largo Caballero’s presence in power, judging his performance in the harshest terms («ineptitud delirante aliada con la traición»), sought to rid himself of him, and was decisive in selecting Juan Negrín, in whom he saw a “tranquil energy” against Prieto’s «altibajos» and «repentes». Prieto made clear to Largo Caballero that the communists’ withdrawal from the cabinet meant the inevitable political crisis.

Teruel y la marcha hacia el Mediterráneo

Teruel and the march to the Mediterranean: the end of the northern front could make observers think the war nearly over. Apart from the rebels’ material and military superiority on land, Soviet supplies were hindered by Italian submarines and the fall of Blum’s government in France; the republican fleet, after losing the Jaime I by accident, acted strictly defensively; the USSR, pressured by Far Eastern events, reduced its aviators in Spain. Everything seemed to favour Franco, yet the fighting was prolonged — at Teruel and the Ebro the German ambassador twice wrote to Hitler that the Spanish conflict had no military solution.

La batalla del Ebro y sus consecuencias: Colapso en Cataluña

The Ebro battle and its consequences — collapse in Catalonia. During the Maestrazgo offensive the Popular Army rebuilt its organisation and effectives when months earlier it had been near disintegration; it prepared a modest offensive that showed its “inconsistency” but revived the desire to resist. Reconstitution drew on new intakes from the “quinta del biberón” (eighteen-year-olds) to reservists over forty — 17 intakes against Franco’s 12. The Army of the Ebro was rebuilt on strict, necessarily political discipline; as Azaña noted, «casi todo el Ejército del Ebro es comunista» — the command (Modesto) and the discipline of the units reflecting it.

Alternativas finales de la política exterior sobre la guerra

Final alternatives of foreign policy on the war: the shifting interventionism of each power and a balance of the aid received by both belligerents in a rapidly changing international conjuncture. The Non-Intervention Committee’s control plan for Spain of early 1937 soon proved ineffective; from April, via the so-called Fagioli agreement, Mussolini’s Italy ceded two submarines — the pattern of covert aid that persisted.

El fin de la guerra

The end of the war: for many Popular Front sympathisers the fall of Catalonia simply meant the end, ratified by France’s and Britain’s immediate recognition of Franco. The final phase is better judged as a phenomenon of disintegration affecting all sectors and protagonists of the Popular Front, who acted in sensibly different ways, than as an interweaving of conspiracies. The first testimony was the surrender of Menorca in the first days of February 1939 — the island and Mahón base had played no important part in the war, the initiative for surrender arising from the simple spectacle of the republican state, with a British warship involved.

Sociedad y cultura en tiempos de guerra

Society and culture in wartime: the polarisation of Spanish society into two or three hard-to-reconcile redoubts was not characteristic of the whole of Spanish history but of the war period and after, since before it divergence had not excluded coexistence. The war opened a deep division destined to last. The decisive factor was partly class, but strictly cultural factors — conceptions of man and of life — probably weighed more than social-sector characterisations; the latifundist aristocracy stood with one side, and culture was mobilised and torn by the conflict.

Relationship to the Wiki

Tusell’s volume II gives the wiki the continuous scholarly history of the Second Republic and the Civil War, the two subjects the wiki’s Spanish contemporary sources approach from within. Where Madariaga surveys Spain from the liberal exile’s standpoint, Chaves Nogales brings the war’s lived texture from the liberal middle, Aznar narrates the campaigns from the Nationalist side, Azaña speaks from inside the 1934 events as the imprisoned accused, Hayes records the American embassy’s view of wartime neutrality, and Fernández Almagro traces the monarchical era that preceded the Republic, Tusell supplies the full analytic arc — the Republic from its 1931 foundation through its reforms, crises, the conservative biennium and October 1934 to the Popular Front of 1936, and the Civil War from conspiracy and revolution through the campaigns, foreign intervention, the internal politics of both zones and the war’s end. His chapters on the revolution, repression and the church sit directly beside Chaves Nogales and Aznar; his analysis of the internationalisation and the powers’ roles beside the German, Italian and Russian wartime sources and Hayes’s American witness; his treatment of the reformist biennium, October 1934 and the Catalan question beside Azaña’s prison memoir. Together with volume III (already held), it makes Tusell the wiki’s scholarly spine for the whole crisis-and-dictatorship sequence of the Spanish twentieth century. Volume IV of the same series (La transición democrática) belongs with the Rest of Europe 1946–present collection when processed.
