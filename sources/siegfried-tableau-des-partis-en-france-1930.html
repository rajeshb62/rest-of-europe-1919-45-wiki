Tableau des partis en France

André Siegfried — Tableau des partis en France (Picture of the Parties in France). Éditions Bernard Grasset, Paris, 1930; a volume in the series “Les Écrits” directed by Jean Guéhenno. A contemporary political geography and sociology of the French party system of the Third Republic, written at the height of the interwar period by the founder of French electoral geography.

AuthorAndré Siegfried (1875–1959) — French political geographer and sociologist, professor at the Collège de France and the École Libre des Sciences Politiques, founder of the electoral-geographical school; author of Tableau politique de la France de l’Ouest sous la troisième République (1913), L’Angleterre d’aujourd’hui (1924) and Les États-Unis d’aujourd’hui (1927); a former parliamentary candidate who turned from candidacy to the systematic study of French politics
Edition1930, Éditions Bernard Grasset, 61 rue des Saints-Pères, Paris; a volume in the collection “Les Écrits” under the direction of Jean Guéhenno; sixth of the second series; printed by Imprimerie Arrault, Tours, 15 September 1930
StructurePreface + 6 parts + 2 appendices, the parts divided into numbered sections; 11,259 lines / ~241,000 characters of extracted text
PeriodThe Third Republic from the mid-nineteenth century to the interwar years, analysed from the standpoint of June 1930: the elections of 1919 (Bloc National), 1924 (Cartel des Gauches) and 1928, and the cabinets of the late 1920s (Poincaré, Tardieu, Chautemps)
NoteA contemporary analytic essay, not a narrative history — Siegfried’s object is to explain what the French parties are and how the French political temperament works, in the tradition of the philosophical-medical “tableau.” He writes explicitly as a sympathetic observer: “mon cœur est à gauche, mais ma poche est à droite” (his heart is on the left, his pocket on the right). For this wiki it supplies the French case that the Russian, German and Italian sources treat from the revolutionary, National Socialist and Fascist sides — the picture of the old parliamentary republic of the centre from within, on the eve of the crises of the 1930s

Contents. A systematic portrait of the French party system and of the temperament underlying it. Part I sketches the permanent French character — France’s place in the contemporary world, the social and economic circumstances that form the French temperament, French civisme (civic-mindedness) and the challenge of industrial evolution. Part II analyses the permanent traits and determining factors of domestic politics: the parties as issuing from the French Revolution and as determined by the Industrial Revolution, and the spirit of French politics (the split between a heart on the left and a pocket on the right). Part III turns to the determining factors of foreign policy — the traditional place of external affairs in French preoccupations, the new internationalist orientation since the Dreyfus affair and the Franco-Russian alliance, and the postwar position. Part IV recounts the cycles of domestic politics since the war: how the balance of parties is struck in France, the Bloc National and the Cartel des Gauches, the Poincaré regime and its comment. Part V spreads out the fan of parties and groups — the Left, the Centre and the Right — and asks where political inspiration in France comes from. Part VI contrasts Latin and Anglo-Saxon democracy: the French conception of government and its survivals from the past, the spirit of the regime under the Third Republic, and the closing comparison of French and Anglo-Saxon democracy, concluding in defence of the individual against the world of mass and series. Two appendices tabulate the relative strength of the parties in the Chamber since the war and the principal votes of 1924–1930.

Character of the source. A contemporary, sympathetic-internal analysis of the Third Republic’s party system, written in 1930 by the man who founded the electoral-geographical study of French politics. It is not a party-aligned tract but the analytical essay of an academic who had himself stood for parliament and abandoned candidacy for the study of politics — “I decided therefore to continue to occupy myself with politics, but in the form of study and observation.” Siegfried’s thesis is that France, an old-formed country complete in its personality since the end of the eighteenth century, faces an industrial world of mass, series and the team that challenges its very individualist civilisation; that the Frenchman is politically on the left and socially conservative; and that the regime’s permanent spirit mixes popular sovereignty, an incorrigible penchant for absolutism and a latent Bonapartism, against which French democracy stands as an effort of liberation of the individual. His stated masters are Seignobos, Robert de Jouvenel, Alain, Étienne Fournol and Albert Thibaudet.

Préface (Preface)

Explains the genesis and method of the book: Siegfried’s earlier Tableau politique de la France de l’Ouest sous la troisième République (1913), intended as the first of a series of regional studies toward “une psychologie politique de la France, basée sur la géographie” (a political psychology of France, based on geography), interrupted by the war; his own experience as a defeated parliamentary candidate (“j’étais un étranger, un exotique”) which taught him the rules of the game by seeing men rather than books; his acknowledgement of his masters — Charles Seignobos, Robert de Jouvenel (La République des camarades), Alain (Essai d’une doctrine radicale), Étienne Fournol, Albert Thibaudet (La République des professeurs) — and of his instinctive sympathy for the regime, even when criticising it; and his closing confession: “La volupté de comprendre me paraît aussi belle que l’ivresse de l’action” (the voluptuousness of understanding seems to me as beautiful as the intoxication of action).

I. Le caractère français (The French Character)

An account of the permanent national temperament that Siegfried holds to underlie the parties.

1. — La France dans le monde actuel (France in the Present-Day World)

France resembles no other country: its conceptions of production, property and individual life are its own, little understood and often isolated in a contemporary world whose code of values is transforming. Economically France is not contemporary with the countries that now set the tone (the United States, Germany, even England are countries of recent economic formation), for the French personality was already complete by the end of the eighteenth century — peasant and artisan come from the middle ages, and “tout l’essentiel était déjà là au moment de la Révolution” (everything essential was already there at the moment of the Revolution). The industrial revolution and the non-European development that displaces the centre of gravity of the planet raise the almost tragic problem of whether France, conceived and built for other circumstances, must change its character and its old political ideology, or whether there is still room for traditional France, including in that tradition the whole development of 1789.

2. — Circonstances sociales qui contribuent à la formation du caractère français (Social Circumstances Contributing to the Formation of the French Character)

The social circumstances — family, the individual, the role of the peasantry — that shape the French temperament and with it the national political disposition.

3. — Circonstances économiques qui contribuent à la formation du caractère français (Economic Circumstances Contributing to the Formation of the French Character)

The economic circumstances — the small producer, the artisan, the protectionist who thinks chiefly of the home market — that form the French character and its politics.

4. — Le caractère français (The French Character)

The synthesis of the French character itself: France as essentially adult, “dure comme ce qui est vieux” (hard as what is old), individualist, set in a world of youth and renewal.

5. — Le civisme français (French Civic-Mindedness)

The peculiar French form of civic spirit and patriotism.

6. — La France devant l’évolution industrielle contemporaine (France Before Contemporary Industrial Evolution)

France before the contemporary industrial evolution: the individual, the team and the series, and the permanent traits of the national temperament that the politics of the Third Republic reflects.

II. Les facteurs déterminants de la politique intérieure (The Determining Factors of Domestic Politics)

The permanent traits and historical determinants of French domestic politics.

1. — Quelques traits permanents (Some Permanent Traits)

The permanent traits of French political behaviour.

2. — Les partis en tant qu’issus de la Révolution française (The Parties as Issuing from the French Revolution)

The parties as issuing from the French Revolution — the founding division of the party system traced to the Revolution and its legacy.

3. — Les partis en tant que déterminés par la Révolution industrielle (The Parties as Determined by the Industrial Revolution)

The parties as determined by the Industrial Revolution — the newer social-economic division superimposed on the revolutionary inheritance.

4. — L’esprit de la politique française (The Spirit of French Politics)

The intimate contradiction of the Frenchman: “son cœur est à gauche, mais sa poche est à droite” (his heart is on the left, but his pocket is on the right) — every Frenchman has a pocket, so that a deferred prudence, his interest, comes sooner or later to contradict the extremist ideology in which he delights; this Don Quixote is always accompanied by a Sancho Panza who never leaves his side. Hence the “toujours à gauche” works only in symbolic gestures, and why France is more really democratic in spirit than the least radical, why foreigners call the French dangerous revolutionaries when they speak and petty bourgeois when they act.

III. Les facteurs déterminants de la politique étrangère (The Determining Factors of Foreign Policy)

The place of external affairs in French politics and its evolution.

1. — Place traditionnelle de la politique extérieure dans les préoccupations françaises (The Traditional Place of Foreign Policy in French Preoccupations)

The traditional place of foreign policy in French preoccupations: economically France is not turned outward — the producer is a protectionist who thinks of the home market, the voter bounded in his local horizon; the export agent, the sailor, the internationally-minded financier are rare exceptions; French deputies, unlike English MPs who take the boat to the Empire, have scarcely travelled the world, and French commercial negotiations think more of defending the market than conquering new ones.

2. — Nouvelle orientation depuis l’affaire Dreyfus (A New Orientation Since the Dreyfus Affair)

The new internationalist orientation since the Dreyfus affair: Michelet’s prophecy that in the twentieth century France would “declare peace to the world” is not the origin of the new ideology, which is foreign — France is losing the ideological initiative it had held unchallenged since the eighteenth century; the Franco-Russian alliance marks an important landmark in the history of political ideas in France.

3. — L’après-guerre (The After-War)

The postwar: the war did not create but amplified existing tendencies; what is truly new since the armistice is that external problems have imposed themselves on France “avec la force irrésistible des questions de salut public” (with the irresistible force of questions of public safety), in Seignobos’s phrase.

IV. Les cycles de notre politique intérieure depuis la guerre (The Cycles of Our Domestic Politics Since the War)

The succession of majority coalitions in the decade after the Great War.

1. — Comment se fait en France la balance des partis (How the Balance of the Parties Is Struck in France)

The mechanism by which the parliamentary balance of parties is produced in France, in light of the intimate contradiction of the Frenchman, politically on the left and socially conservative.

2. — Le bloc national et le cartel des gauches (The National Bloc and the Left Cartel)

The two great majority cycles of the 1920s. In 1919, the recent image of war impressed less than the menacing shadow of bolshevism that terrified the bourgeois; there was a resentment of the combatants against the deputies, antiparliamentary effluvia in the air more insistent than since Boulangism or the Panama affair, and a feeling that the political militant had lost prestige to the technician — the creation of the war who, under the name of expert, was to reappear in innumerable international conferences. The Left, which stood for the worker, the deputy and the militant, suffered by solidarity — “La guerre ne vous a donc rien appris?” (Has the war then taught you nothing?). Against this stands the Cartel des Gauches of 1924.

3. — Le régime Poincaré (The Poincaré Regime)

The regime of Raymond Poincaré — the stabilisation and the conservative-radical governments of the later 1920s.

4. — Commentaire (Commentary)

A general commentary drawing together the significance of the postwar cycles.

V. L’éventail des partis et des groupes (The Fan of the Parties and Groups)

After the balance of forces, “il nous reste à envisager la couleur des opinions” (we have still to envisage the colour of the opinions): the fan of parties spread across the political spectrum.

1. — La gauche (The Left)

The Left — the socialists and radicals of the republican spectrum.

2. — Le centre (The Centre)

The Centre — the moderate republican and radical-Socialist middle of the spectrum.

3. — La droite (The Right)

The Right — the conservative and nationalist parties of the spectrum.

4. — D’où vient l’inspiration politique en France (Whence Comes Political Inspiration in France)

Where political inspiration in France comes from — the sources of doctrine and opinion that feed the parties.

VI. Démocratie latine et démocratie anglo-saxonne (Latin Democracy and Anglo-Saxon Democracy)

The closing comparison of the French with the Anglo-Saxon conception of democracy.

1. — Conceptions de gouvernement et survivances du passé (Conceptions of Government and Survivals of the Past)

The French, it is said, renounce their past — in words; and yet that past survives: the trace of the old regimes is everywhere in French conceptions of government, so that to analyse them is to make a retrospective exhibition.

2. — L’esprit du régime sous la troisième république (The Spirit of the Regime Under the Third Republic)

Three persistences and three adaptations or reactions determine the permanent factors of the regime. The persistences: the affirmation of popular sovereignty (to be considered irrevocable); an incorrigible penchant for absolutism, the tenacious virus of the past that reappears nine times out of ten in those who administer or govern (“le pouvoir, dit Alain, est toujours monarchique” — power, says Alain, is always monarchical); and the Bonapartist spirit, this nostalgia for power in a regime that systematically enervates power — hence a latent antiparliamentarism, a paradoxical spectacle in a country that precisely willed parliamentarism to be everything. The adaptations or reactions are as vigorous as the causes that provoke them: because the people wish to be everything, their elected delegate must become the essential piece of the system — hence the supremacy of the deputy, of universal suffrage; and because government is enervated by its very nature…

3. — Démocratie française et démocratie anglo-saxonne (French Democracy and Anglo-Saxon Democracy)

The closing comparison. The end pursued by the French in democracy is essentially to emancipate the individual — intellectually by laicism, politically by resistance to “tyranny”: the will of each not to let himself be dominated by some other individual whose right to command is contested. In the popular preoccupation it remains secondary whether the regime produces more well-being, more comfort, a higher standard of living. Here touches the very essence of the Latin conception: democracy, like the gospel, becomes above all a state of mind, a will to liberation always awake, “un effort continuel, qui détruit à chaque instant un peu de monarchie renaissante” (a continual effort, which destroys at each instant a little of the re-born monarchy). The book closes by defending this old French political mystique — with its ridicules, its insufficiency of social output — as justified and explained only by an instinctive will to preserve the individual, the vital instinct that France will, no doubt, never renounce: should the question of the individual, his rights and the imprescriptible freedom of his springing-forth become again a preoccupation of men, it is not with a vocabulary of vacuum cleaners, frigidaires and calculating machines that the world will be raised — the French ideology, its dynamism intact, will recover all its power.

Appendice I. Force respective des partis à la Chambre depuis la guerre (The Respective Strength of the Parties in the Chamber Since the War)

Statistical tables of party strength in the three postwar Chambers: the Bloc National Chamber of 1919–1924 (617 deputies — Left 180: 68 socialists, 24 socialist republicans, 88 radical-Socialists; Centre 216; Right 221: 185 progressists, 36 independents); the Cartel Chamber of 1924–1928 (582 deputies — Left 328: 105 united socialists, 42 socialist republicans, 140 radical-Socialists, 41 radical Left; Centre 80; Right 146: 104 democratic republican Union, 14 democrats, 28 independents; 28 Communists); and the Chamber of 1928–1932 (611 deputies, groups at their June 1930 strengths — Left 271: 107 united socialists, 114 radical-Socialists, 16 socialist republicans, 14 members of the Socialist Party…).

Appendice II. Quelques scrutins importants 1924–1930 (Some Important Votes, 1924–1930)

A record of the principal division votes of 1924–1930: the overthrow of the François-Marsal cabinet (10 June 1924; Left 327, Centre and Right 217); the weakening of the Herriot cabinet (9 April 1925; for Herriot 290 in the “petit cartel,” against 242, the radical Left abstaining); the Caillaux vote of confidence (12 July 1925); the fall of the Tardieu ministry (17 February 1930) and of the Chautemps cabinet (25 February 1930), and other decisive scrutinies of the period.
