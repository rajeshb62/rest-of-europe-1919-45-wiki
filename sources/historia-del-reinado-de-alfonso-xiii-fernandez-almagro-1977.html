Melchor Fernández Almagro — Historia del reinado de Alfonso XIII

Full bibliographic citation — Melchor Fernández Almagro (1893–1966). Historia del reinado de Alfonso XIII. — 4th edition. Montaner y Simón, S. A., Barcelona, 1977. © Montaner y Simón, S. A.; ISBN 84-274-379-8; Depósito legal B. 19.049-1977 (Gráficas Román, S. A.). The edition is the mature synthesis of a historian-critic of Granada, a member of the Real Academia de la Historia, whose chronicle of the political history of contemporary Spain Azorín praised as “minucioso, exacto y sugestivo”.

Fernández Almagro’s Historia del reinado de Alfonso XIII is a strictly political and external history of Spain from the accession of Alfonso XIII (17 May 1902) to the proclamation of the Republic (14 April 1931). In his Nota preliminar the author declares the work’s method and scope: “Ha aspirado a hacer historia. Esto es: a contar las cosas como han sido” — to tell things as they were, “ni el vejamen ni la apología de nada ni de nadie” (neither the vilification nor the apology of anything or anyone). He insists that the difficulty of writing contemporary history lies not in any lack of perspective but in the poverty of Spanish sources — the scarcity of memoirs, the destruction of family papers, the State’s own failure to preserve archives or publish documents — and affirms that perspective can be obtained “a despecho de la cronología, mediante un esfuerzo moral”. He identifies the work as “política y externa”, political and external, giving the general march of the State as the skeleton to which internal history (culture, economy, customs) must be attached, and observes that between 1902 and 1931 the society of Spain was so thoroughly renewed that “la sociedad y el Estado… siguieron caminos distintos” — two Spains joined only by an ascending line of wealth and enlightenment. The body is a continuous narrative chronicle, built as a sequence of dated sections and governments rather than thematic chapters, divided into six Parts.

Content Summary

Front matter. Decorated title leaf (the print title is only partly legible in the scan); imprint leaf identifying the fourth edition, © Montaner y Simón, S. A., ISBN and Depósito legal; then the Nota preliminar (pp. 5–11), the author’s statement of method and a synthesis of the whole reign as five successive periods: the liquidation of the regency (1902–1906), the renewal intents of Maura and Canalejas (to 1912), the unstable equilibrium of the war years (to 1917), the revolutionary attempts (1917–1923), and the Dictatorship (1923–1930), closing in the monarchy’s capitulation (1930–1931).

Part I — Liquidación de la Regencia (1902–1906)

1. Sagasta (1902). “17 de mayo de 1902. — Sagasta, presidente. — Primer Consejo de ministros.” The king’s first ministry and its enmities — palatines, enemies of the régime, socialism, Canalejas’s resignation and propaganda — the royal journey, Sagasta and the new problems, the religious associations, public instruction, the crisis and Sagasta’s fall.

2. Silvela (1902–1903). Two governments: Silvela’s — Morocco, the liberals, Unión Republicana, the Salamanca events, the Marina conflict, Villaverde’s resignation, elections, the king and Maura, the twenty-six dead, the opening of the Cortes — then the Villaverde government, Alcalá del Valle, Silvela’s withdrawal, the Bilbao strike, the king and the military, and Villaverde’s fall.

3. Maura (1903–1904). First Maura government — the budget, the press, the Nozaleda question, the removal of Admiral Beránger, republican propaganda, the moderating power, the Vigo meeting of William II and Alfonso XIII, the Franco-English accord and the Franco-Spanish treaty, the agreement with Rome, the king in Barcelona, Catalan politics, the attempt on Maura, the royal visit to the Balearics, África and Andalusia, the scandalous crisis and the succession to the Throne.

4. Dos puentes (1904–1905). The interlude governments of Azcárraga and Villaverde — the ministerial declaration, whether toward a coup d’état, official favour, procuring the Cortes, famine in Andalusia, the students and La Cierva, the sinking of the third depot, the king’s journey to France and England, the attempt in Paris, London, Maura and Villaverde, and the rise of the villaverdistas.

5. Cinco gobiernos liberales (1905–1907). Five liberal ministries — Montero Ríos (the Barcelona events, military imposition, royal intervention, his fall), Moret (the king’s military policy, the Algeciras conference, the Law of Jurisdictions, the king’s marriage, the attempt by Morral against the royal couple), López Domínguez (anticlerical policy, the Bishops’ protest, Canalejas’s note, the Law of Associations), the “relámpago” (lightning) government, and Vega de Armijo — the “papelito” crisis and the fall into Maura’s second accession.

Part II — Intentos de Renovación (1907–1912)

1. Gobierno largo de Maura (1907–1909). The long Maura government — a new style, the movement toward the right, terrorism, Solidarios and antisolidarios, the republicans and Salmerón, the Cartagena meeting of Edward VII and Alfonso XIII, the general elections and the liberals’ withdrawal, the Catalan theme, five laws and the Rull affair, the local-administration project, Cambó and the Lliga, the Escuadra law, the anti-terrorism project, the king in Barcelona; then the “año memorable” — Morocco and its antecedents, the occupation of the Restinga and Cabo de Agua, the action of 9 July, the Beni-Bu-Ifrur mines, the call-up of reservists, La Cierva’s attitude, the battle of the Gurugú and the Barranco del Lobo, the “Semana trágica” of Barcelona, the trial and execution of Francisco Ferrer Guardia and the Escola Moderna, the foreign protests and the “mito” Ferrer, Moret against Maura, and the parliamentary crisis.

2. Paréntesis. The Moret government — the republican-socialist conjunción, Maura’s “implacable hostilidad”, the “¡Maura, sí!”, Morocco, and militarism.

3. Canalejas (1910–1912). Canalejas’s presidency — his programme, the new Cortes, republicans and socialists in the Senate, the royal message, Maura, Pablo Iglesias and an attempt, social questions, the Bilbao strike, the religious question, Morocco, the Cullera events and the Kert campaign, the battle of 27 December and the death of El-Mizzian, the Cullera trial, the crisis, the law of the Mancomunidades provinciales, the railway strike, the mobilisation of reservists, and the assassination of Canalejas.

Part III — Equilibrio Inestable (1912–1917)

1. Romanones (1912–1913). Two aspirants to Canalejas’s succession; the interlude of García Prieto, then Romanones president of the Council — the Franco-Spanish treaty on Morocco and its ratification, Maura’s retirement and the conservative party, the republicans applauding the king, liberal concessions, the occupation of Tetuán, Alfonso XIII in Paris, Poincaré in Madrid and Cartagena, the Mancomunidades in the Senate, the total crisis, the adhesion of the reformists to the monarchy, and Dato’s accession to power.

2. Dato (1913–1915). A typical conservative — Maura at Guisando, Sánchez-Guerra in Gobernación, “idóneos” and “mauristas”, the king’s unwillingness to be spoken to sharply, the king and the Army, the king and the left, Morocco, the Mannesmann brothers, Lyautey in Madrid, the Decree of the Mancomunidades and the Mancomunidad of Cataluña, general elections, the Liga de Educación Política and Ortega y Gasset, the Spanish neutrality in the European war, “neutralidades que matan”, the king at the ready, Lerroux as interventionist, the Junta de Iniciativas, the German-maritime-blockade note, the sinking of Spanish ships, Catalan separatism, and the “crisis del susto”.

3. Segundo gobierno romanonés (1915–1917). Romanones’s second ministry — the new ministry, Basque and Catalan nationalisms, general elections and Azcárate’s defeat, the royal message, the railway conflict, a German submarine at Cartagena, French pressures and the sinking of Spanish ships, the pro-German press, diplomatic notes and the blockade declaration, the law of authorisations, a conference on Morocco and the attempt at a general strike, the torpedoing of the San Fulgencio, and the total “crisis verdadera”.

Part IV — Conatos Revolucionarios (1917–1923)

1. García Prieto: las Juntas de Defensa. García Prieto president, ratification of neutrality, the note to Germany, the military movement and its antecedents, the constitution of the Juntas de Defensa, the 1 June exposition, the king, Weyler and the Juntas, the approval of Article 1, a national crisis, the mauristas and the king, revolutionary stimuli, and the beginnings of the Russian revolution.

2. Dato: la Asamblea de parlamentarios y la huelga de agosto (1917). Dato’s second government, approval of the Junta regulation, the revolutionary accord of republicans and socialists, the Asamblea de parlamentarios in Barcelona, the general revolutionary strike — in Madrid, in the provinces, in Barcelona — the Juntas and the revolution, the message to the king, the lanzamiento (overthrow) of Dato, the eight-day crisis, the Asamblea at the Ateneo of Madrid, Cambó in the palace, toward a minimal renovation.

3. Primer gobierno de concentración (1917–1918). García Prieto’s concentration government — ministerial dissonance, municipal elections, the lure of Russia, public order and subsistence, La Cierva and his military policy, general elections, the grant by royal decree of military reforms, another crisis, the civil Juntas, the postal and telegraph strike, plans of military dictatorship, an idea of Romanones, and the national government.

4. Gobierno nacional (1918). A moment of illusion and a minimum programme — the end of the communications strike, reticence against the Army, nationalism in the Cortes, the debate on the August strike, the anti-espionage law and the left, torpedoed ships and Spain’s reclamation, the Germanophile ballast, the confiscation of six German ships, meetings, strikes and the influenza, the new ministry of Abastecimientos, Cambó’s project and Alba’s plans and resignation, the total crisis and the eve of the armistice.

5. Gobiernos y gobiernos: el sindicalismo (1918–1921). A sequence of short ministries — García Prieto again; Romanones (the Catalan question, the parliamentary withdrawal of the catalanistas, the Sindicato Único, the “La Canadiense” strike, strikes throughout Spain, José Moróte’s mediation, Ángel Pestaña and the Noi del Sucre, the Juntas expelling the civil governor and police chief of Barcelona); Maura (the telegraph strike, the dissolution of the Cortes, the consecration of Spain to the Sacred Heart, the left against Maura); Sánchez de Toca (social war, the Federación Patronal, Bravo Portillo, the students of the Superior War School); Allendesalazar (the military revolt in Zaragoza, Milans del Bosch and Salvatierra de Álava, Weyler in Barcelona, the budget); and Dato again (the creation of the Labour ministry, Joffre in Barcelona, the recrudecimiento of the social war, Tafersit and Xauen, Martínez Anido governor of Barcelona, the CNT and the UGT, the “ley de fugas”, the theme of repression in the Congress) — ending with Dato’s assassination and Bugallal as interim president.

6. Annual (1921–1922). The Moroccan catastrophe — the government and Martínez Anido, terrorism in the Congress and in action, the king’s Córdoba speech, the crisis of socialism and the formation of the communist party, the collapse of the Melilla command, Berenguer and Fernández Silvestre, the king and Silvestre, Abd-El-Krim, Monte Abarran, the disaster of Igueriben and the disaster of Annual, the stupefaction of Spain, the defence of Melilla, the “República del Rif”, the fall of the government; then Maura (confidence in Berenguer, the Picasso inquiry, the spirit of the public, the advance, Tizza, the macabre landscapes, Gomara, the informative commissions, Dar-Drius, the Pizarra conference, Unamuno against the king); and Sánchez-Guerra (restoration of constitutional guarantees, terrorism in Barcelona, Tazarut, responsibilities and rewards, the Picasso expediente in the Supreme Council of War and Navy, Berenguer’s prosecution and resignation, the Congress debate, the dissolution of the Juntas, the destitution of Martínez Anido and Arlegui, fascism, the parliamentary commission and the expediente Picasso).

7. Ultimo gobierno constitucional (1922–1923). The liberal Concentration in power — a royal decree of Romanones, general elections, military unease, the rescue of prisoners, the artillerymen, civil responsibilities, terrorism and the assassination of the Noi del Sucre, the transport strike, Alcalá-Zamora’s resignation, Martínez Anido commander-general of Melilla, Primo de Rivera in Madrid, the attitude and thought of the king, the denounced conspiracy, the commission of twenty-one deputies, a government plan for Morocco, the siege of Tifaruin, the sedition in Málaga, the Central Staff memorandum, the king’s consultation with Maura, the date of the pronunciamiento, the Captaincy-General of Cataluña, the king and Alba, Aizpuru and Primo de Rivera, the government’s isolation, Primo de Rivera’s impatience — and Primo de Rivera on the way.

Part V — La Dictadura (1923–1930)

1. Directorio militar (1923–1925). The dictator and the naming of the Military Directory — opinions and attitudes, Primo de Rivera and Catalanism, the first measures of the Dictatorship, Martínez Anido subsecretary of Gobernación, the dictator and the socialists, against the politicians, the journey to Rome and Mussolini and Primo de Rivera, the Tánger Statute, the municipal statute, the exile of the Marqués de Cortina and the sanctions against the Duque de San Pedro de Galatino, the Caoba episode, the confinement of Unamuno and Soriáno, the responsibilities of 1921 and the amnesty, the Unión Patriótica, Morocco and the line of the Lau, the general rising of the cabilas, Lyautey’s declarations and the fear of another Annual, the evacuation, Abd-El-Krim against the Raisuli and against France, the Hispano-French conference of Madrid, Primo de Rivera and Pétain, the landing at Alhucemas, the operations, the hour to leave power, the students and the attempt of Colonel Segundo García, and the substitution of the military Dictatorship by a civil one.

2. Gobierno de hombres civiles (1925–1930). The civil ministry — the new ministers and first measures, the flight of the Plus Ultra, sanctions on La Época, the foundation of Alianza Republicana, the “Sanjuanada”, the extraordinary fines, the 1926 spring campaign in Morocco, the battle of the Loma de los Morabos, the surrender of Abd-El-Krim, the artillery pronunciamiento, Spain in the League of Nations, the plebiscite toward a consultative Assembly, the Prats de Molló intent, the “raid” of Commander Capaz; 1927 — the end of the campaign, the convocation and opening of the National Consultative Assembly, the exile of Sánchez-Guerra; 1928 — the social question, schools, the socialists thinking of the end of the Dictatorship, the debates in the Assembly, the ministerial council at Coruña; 1929 — Sánchez-Guerra in Valencia, the Ciudad Real pronunciamiento and the failure of the movement, the dissolution of the Artillery, the death of Doña María Cristina, the student strike against Article 53 of the University Statute, the Sevillian and Barcelona Expositions, the constitutional draft, the depreciation of the peseta, the enemies of the Dictatorship; and 1930 — the preparation of a pronunciamiento in Cádiz, the result of the consultation to the Army, the abortive pronunciamiento, the end of the Dictatorship, Berenguer the new head of government, and the last official note of Primo de Rivera.

Part VI — Capitulación de la Monarquía (1930–1931)

1. Berenguer. The old parties and the new ministers, toward constitutional normality, the last acts and death of Primo de Rivera, the Unión Monárquica Nacional, Sánchez-Guerra’s speech at the Zarzuela, Alcalá-Zamora’s speech at Valencia, Unamuno in Madrid, Ossorio demanding the king’s abdication and Sánchez Román’s bill of indictment, the military-republican association’s circular, the syndicalists, the communists, the catalanistas and the intellectuals of Madrid, the republicans, the decree on regional flags, the electoral-census renewal, Cambó and Alba and the king, the peseta, the Pact of San Sebastián, propaganda, counter-propaganda and strikes, the impatience of the CNT, the abortive movement, the general strike in Madrid, the military rebellion at Jaca, Captain Galán and Anzánigo–Ayerbe–Cillas, the execution of Galán and García Hernández, the manifesto of the provisional government of the projected Spanish Republic, the Cuatro Vientos airfield, renewed conspiracy and the Agrupación al Servicio de la República, the parties before the electoral convocation, general abstention, and the total crisis.

2. La Monarquía, sitiada. “La realidad puede más que la realeza” — consultations, Sánchez-Guerra called to power, the meeting at the ministry of War, the Aznar government and its ministerial declaration, the council of war at Jaca and at Madrid, the revolutionary committee freed, the opening of a credit at the Morgan Bank, the Madrid faculty-of-Medicine events, the amnesty, the Centro Constitucional, before the municipal elections, the municipal elections of 12 April, the triumph of the republican-socialist coalition, the eviction of the monarchy, the interview of Romanones and Alcalá-Zamora, the proclamation of the Republic, the last Council of Ministers, the provisional government of the Republic at the ministry of Gobernación, and the departure of Don Alfonso.

The volume closes with the Índice (table of contents, pp. 480–488) and the 1977 jacket text on the character, the epoch and the author.

Relationship to the Wiki

Fernández Almagro’s reign-history is the closest thing in the wiki to an institutional Spanish history of the years 1902–1931: it is written from the standpoint of a Spanish historian who took the monarchy’s constitutional machinery as its subject, tracing each government, each crisis and each royal intervention from Sagasta to the exit of Don Alfonso. It therefore complements the wiki’s existing Spanish sources — Madariaga’s exile liberal case, Chaves Nogales and Aznar on the Civil War, Azaña on 1934, Hayes on wartime neutrality, Tusell on the Republic and Civil War, and Tusell on the Franco dictatorship that closed the monarchical and republican sequences — by supplying the preceding monarchical era and its long Moroccan and domestic crises, and it connects to the interwar themes already present: the Russian revolution’s echo (the Juntas, the general strike of 1917), the rise of fascism (the Mussolini–Primo de Rivera encounter), and the collapse of the liberal order that the European sources describe from their own national standpoints.
