L’Étrange Défaite

Marc Bloch — L’Étrange Défaite (Strange Defeat). Written July–September 1940; first published posthumously by Éditions Franc-Tireur, Paris, 1946; the digital edition used here (Feedbooks) reproduces the full volume, which appends to L’Étrange Défaite proper the Testament de Marc Bloch and the Écrits clandestins. The celebrated eyewitness account of the collapse of France in 1940 by one of the foremost French historians of the century.

AuthorMarc Bloch (1886–1944) — French medieval historian, co-founder with Lucien Febvre of the Annales school and the journal Annales d’histoire économique et sociale (1929); author of Les Rois thaumaturges (1924), Les Caractères originaux de l’histoire rurale française (1931) and La Société féodale (1939–40); veteran of both World Wars — corporal in 1914, mobilised again as a captain of the Intelligence staff (3e Bureau) in 1939; after the defeat a professor at Montpellier, deprived of his chair by the Vichy racial laws, who entered the Resistance under the pseudonym “Narbonne,” was captured, tortured and shot by the Germans on 16 June 1944 near Lyon
EditionFeedbooks digital reproduction of the full Franc-Tireur volume; L’Étrange Défaite written in a few weeks between July and September 1940 (the manuscript closes “Guéret-Fougères (Creuse): juillet-septembre 1940”), published 1946 with an avant-propos by Georges Altman; the volume also contains Bloch’s Testament (Clermont-Ferrand, 18 March 1941) and the posthumous Écrits clandestins; 7,553 lines / ~460,000 characters of extracted text
StructureAvant-propos by Georges Altman + Partie 1, L’Étrange Défaite (3 chapters: “Présentation du témoin,” “La déposition d’un vaincu,” “Examen de conscience d’un Français”) + Partie 2, Le Testament de Marc Bloch + Partie 3, Écrits clandestins (6 essays, chiefly dating from 1941–43)
PeriodThe French collapse of May–June 1940 and its causes, judged at the moment of the defeat (July–September 1940); the appended clandestine essays written under Vichy, 1941–1943
NoteThe testimony of a professional historian writing “en pleine rage” (in a fury) in the weeks after the disaster, who declares he writes not his memoirs but a procès-verbal (an official report) of the year 1940 for future researchers. For this wiki it supplies the French case from the side of the victim and the analyst — the internal diagnosis of the “strange defeat” from a citizen-soldier of the Third Republic’s academic elite, standing at the opposite pole from the German, Italian and Russian sources presented from the totalitarian side

Contents. Georges Altman’s avant-propos praises the work as “un récit, une explication, un réquisitoire” (a narrative, an explanation, an indictment) without equal in France on 1940, and the voice of “un grand civil martyr, mort sans avoir jamais douté de l’aube” (a great civilian martyr, dead without ever having doubted the dawn). L’Étrange Défaite itself, dated July–September 1940, is divided into three chapters. “Présentation du témoin” (Presentation of the Witness) gives Bloch’s état civil as a witness: thirty-four years a historian, twice a soldier, and the first duty of the historian, in his master Pirenne’s phrase, to be interested “à la vie” (in life). “La déposition d’un vaincu” (The Deposition of a Defeated Man) — the longest chapter, written first and in the heat of the moment — recounts the collapse as Bloch saw it from the Intelligence staff of the 1st Army: the unreadiness of the high command, the confusion, the rout, the extraordinary slowness of the French command to grasp the war that the Germans were fighting. “Examen de conscience d’un Français” (A Frenchman’s Examination of Conscience) — written with more distance after the armistice — turns from the army to the nation: the responsibility of the professional military caste, of the political class, of the bourgeoisie, of the educational system, of the whole French society that had failed to prepare for total war. Partie 2 is Bloch’s Testament (18 March 1941): his instructions for his own burial, his refusal of religious ceremony, his affirmation that he was born a Jew, never sought to deny it and found no reason to be tempted to do so, and his declaration that throughout his life he felt himself “avant tout et très simplement Français” (above all and very simply French). Partie 3, the Écrits clandestins, gathers seven essays written under Vichy: “Pourquoi je suis républicain,” “L’alimentation humaine et les échanges internationaux,” “La vraie saison des juges,” “Un philosophe de bonne compagnie,” “À propos d’un livre trop peu connu,” “Sur la réforme de l’enseignement,” and the closing reflection on the France of the Resistance.

Character of the source. A first-person testimony and self-examination written in the weeks immediately following the catastrophe of 1940, by a man who combined the exacting method of the professional historian with the lived experience of two wars. Bloch is explicit about his purpose: “Je n’écris pas ici mes souvenirs” (I am not writing my memoirs here) — he writes a witness’s report, intended to lie hidden until the day France “verra de nouveau s’épanouir… la liberté de pensée et de jugement” (again sees the freedom of thought and judgment blossom), when researchers may profit from “ce procès-verbal de l’an 1940” (this report of the year 1940). It is at once a narrative of defeat, an analysis of its causes reaching into the deepest structures of French society and the French military mind, and a moral examination of conscience. His enemies are the routine of the military caste, the mental timidity of the ruling classes, the “vieux démon” of the bourgeoisie, and the intellectual smugness that left France unprepared; his heroes are the common people of France — the miners of the Nord and the Pas-de-Calais whom he names the best warriors of 1914–18, “une aristocratie” of honesty and courage. Written in the present tense of its own moment, it is a contemporary source of the first rank for the defeat of 1940 and the French reaction to it.

Avant-propos de Georges Altman (Preface by Georges Altman)

Georges Altman’s preface praises the work: “il n’a pas paru à ce jour en France, sur 1940, un récit, une explication, un réquisitoire d’une pensée aussi lucide, d’un dessin aussi net” (there has appeared to this day in France, on 1940, no narrative, explanation or indictment of so lucid a thought, of so clear a design). Bloch wrote the text, he says, “en pleine rage” (in a fury) — “la belle rage d’un grand esprit qui n’admet pas” (the fine fury of a great mind that does not accept). Altman stresses the second part, the examen de conscience: “la bouleversante confession d’un grand intellectuel français qui se penche sans merci sur un monde et sur une caste” (the overwhelming confession of a great French intellectual who bends without mercy over a world and a caste). He closes by citing Bloch’s tribute to the common people: “Je n’ai pas connu en 1914-1918 de meilleurs guerriers que les mineurs du Nord ou du Pas-de-Calais” (I knew no better warriors in 1914–1918 than the miners of the Nord or the Pas-de-Calais), with the single exception of a man who proved to be a jaune, a non-unionised strike-breaker — evidence, for Bloch, that political choice, not class, made the soldier.

Partie 1 — L’Étrange Défaite (Strange Defeat)

The main work, written July–September 1940.

Chapitre 1 — Présentation du témoin (Presentation of the Witness)

Bloch declares he has decided to write, though the pages will long be unknown save “sous le manteau” (under the cloak): “Un témoignage ne vaut que fixé dans sa première fraîcheur” (a testimony is worth only fixed in its first freshness). He gives his état civil — “Écrire et enseigner l’histoire: tel est, depuis tantôt trente-quatre ans, mon métier” (to write and teach history: such has been, for some thirty-four years now, my trade) — and the first duty of the historian, in Pirenne’s phrase, to be interested in life: “sans se pencher sur le présent, il est impossible de comprendre le passé” (without bending over the present, it is impossible to understand the past). He was twice a soldier: he began the first war in August 1914 as a sergeant of infantry, “presque au niveau du simple soldat” (almost at the level of the simple soldier), and rose through chef de section, officer of intelligence, to captain; in 1939, twenty-one years later, he was mobilised again into the Intelligence staff.

Chapitre 2 — La déposition d’un vaincu (The Deposition of a Defeated Man)

“We have just suffered an incredible defeat. Whose fault is it? The parliamentary regime, the troops, the English, the …” — so the second chapter opens, listing the rival recriminations. The account of the collapse from within the Intelligence staff of the 1st Army: the high command’s unreadiness, its “extraordinary slowness” to grasp the nature of the war the Germans were fighting, the confusion and the rout, the collapse of communications and command, the breakdown of the army as a fighting institution. Bloch the historian applies to the present the same habits of criticism, observation and honesty he had brought to old documents: he is a witness who also explains, measuring the “strange defeat” against the standards of military history and of the German conduct of war.

Chapitre 3 — Examen de conscience d’un Français (A Frenchman’s Examination of Conscience)

The closing chapter, written with greater distance after the armistice, turns from the army to the nation: “Dans une nation, jamais aucun corps professionnel n’est, à lui seul, responsable” (in a nation, no professional body is ever alone responsible). The failure of the professional military caste, of the political class, of the bourgeoisie and its “vieux démon,” of the educational system and the whole society that had not prepared itself for total war; the “examen de conscience” of a Frenchman who refuses the facile explanation and indicts the deep structure of the Third Republic. The manuscript closes: “Guéret-Fougères (Creuse): juillet-septembre 1940.”

Partie 2 — Le Testament de Marc Bloch (The Testament of Marc Bloch)

Bloch’s testament, dated “Clermont-Ferrand, le 18 mars 1941.” He leaves to his wife, or failing her to his children, the care of his funeral: “Ce seront des obsèques purement civiles” (they will be purely civil obsequies), with no Hebrew prayers despite their cadences having accompanied so many of his ancestors and his father himself — his whole life he had striven “vers une sincérité totale de l’expression et de l’esprit” (towards a total sincerity of expression and spirit), and he held complaisance in lying to be the worst leprosy of the soul; his device is “Dilexit veritatem” (he loved truth). But it would be more odious still that any see in this act of probity a cowardly renunciation: “J’affirme donc, s’il le faut, face à la mort, que je suis né Juif” (I affirm then, if need be, in the face of death, that I was born a Jew) — the generous tradition of the Hebrew prophets, taken up and widened by Christianity in its purest, remains “une de nos meilleures raisons de vivre, de croire et de lutter” (one of our best reasons to live, to believe and to fight). “Étranger à tout formalisme confessionnel comme à toute solidarité prétendument raciale, je me suis senti, durant ma vie entière, avant tout et très simplement Français” (a stranger to all confessional formalism as to all supposedly racial solidarity, I have felt myself, during my whole life, above all and very simply French) — “je meurs, comme j’ai vécu, en bon Français” (I die, as I lived, a good Frenchman). He asks that his five military citations be read at his grave.

Partie 3 — Écrits clandestins (Clandestine Writings)

Seven essays written under Vichy and gathered in this posthumous volume.

Chapitre 1 — Pourquoi je suis républicain (Why I Am a Republican)

“Asking me why I am a republican, is that not already to be one? Is it not to admit that the form of power can be an object of choice maturely deliberated by the citizen, that the community therefore does not impose itself on man…” — Bloch’s republican confession under the Vichy regime that had abolished the Republic.

Chapitre 2 — L’alimentation humaine et les échanges internationaux (Human Food and International Exchanges)

An essay on food supply and international trade, reflecting Bloch’s wartime and blockade-era concerns.

Chapitre 3 — La vraie saison des juges (The True Season of Judges)

On law, judgment and the judges — written under a regime that had made law a weapon.

Chapitre 4 — Un philosophe de bonne compagnie (A Philosopher of Good Company)

A literary-philosophical essay, presumably on a figure of French thought.

Chapitre 5 — À propos d’un livre trop peu connu (On a Book Too Little Known)

A note drawing attention to a neglected work.

Chapitre 6 — Sur la réforme de l’enseignement (On the Reform of Education)

Bloch’s notes “pour une révolution de l’enseignement” (for a revolution in education), written for the Cahiers politiques of the resistance: “Tout malheur national appelle, d’abord, un examen de conscience” (every national misfortune calls, first, for an examination of conscience), then a plan of renovation. He demands a reasoned revision of values throughout — an historical and geographical teaching “largement conçu” and, for history at least, “totalement refondu” (totally recast), giving the young a truthful and comprehensive image of the world; a warning against reducing history to the purely political events of a Europe temporally close to us — “le passé lointain inspire le sens et le respect des différences entre les hommes” (the distant past inspires the sense and respect of differences between men) — and it matters more to a future French citizen to form a just image of the civilisations of India or China than to know by heart the succession of measures by which the “autoritaire Empire” became the “libéral Empire.” The tradition française, “incorporée dans un long destin pédagogique,” is dear to him; they intend to preserve its most precious goods — its taste for the human, its respect for spiritual spontaneity and liberty, the continuity of the forms of art and thought that are the very climate of the French spirit — knowing that to be truly faithful to it commands them to prolong it towards the future.
