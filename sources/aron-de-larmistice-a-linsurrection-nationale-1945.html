De l’armistice à l’insurrection nationale

Raymond Aron — De l’armistice à l’insurrection nationale (From the Armistice to the National Insurrection). Librairie Gallimard, Paris, 1945, 2e édition. A collected volume of the London La France Libre “Chroniques de France” written between November 1940 and May 1944 — month-by-month analysis, from Free France abroad, of Vichy, Occupation, collaboration and the nation’s path toward resistance and national uprising.

AuthorRaymond Aron (1905–1983) — French philosopher, sociologist and political commentator; author of Introduction à la philosophie de l’histoire and, from exile, L’Homme contre les tyrans (New York); in London a leading editorial voice of La France Libre, reading Occupied and Vichy France for an audience largely foreign, while Free France fought and waited outside the métropole

Contents. A continuous series of dated “Chroniques de France” first published in La France Libre (London). The Préface (1945) explains the London vantage, the fragmentary documentation, and the wish to stay short of interior résistants’ violence of judgment while refusing to confuse traitors with the many French of good faith who served Vichy in 1940. Introduction / I — La Capitulation (15 November 1940) opens on Reynaud’s last appeal and Pétain’s call to cease combat, then analyses actors, the collapse of the regime, French resignation, official justifications, and the false choice between “politique du moindre mal” and continuation of the war. Première Partie — Attentisme follows the Vichy years of wait-and-see: material reconstruction and moral atmosphere under the line of demarcation; the new regime’s men and ideas; the “organisation of misery”; government of notables; food supply; propaganda and opinion; culture and society; collaboration as exploitation one year after the armistice; the slide from notables’ rule to a police regime; étatisme and corporation; finances of defeat; the Riom trial as a “defy to Hitler”; Laval’s return and attempted mise au pas; military lessons of Riom; the “political comedy” of rival collaborationist ideologies; legalisation of plunder; and the new German technique of orders, industrial concentration and labour conscription. Deuxième Partie — Collaboration opens after November 1942 (total occupation, scuttling of the fleet, empire back in the war): disintegration of the Vichy regime; aggravated food and transport crisis; collaboration versus abstention; the Charlemagne-empire myth versus Richelieu’s testament; economic chaos; deportations and famine; impoverishment of the nation; end of ambiguities (from Pétainisme to Resistance, from Légion to Milice); last illusions; “gangsters in power” (Darnand); and, on the eve of D-Day, Vers la deuxième bataille de France (Déat, Henriot, Darnand). The Note finale (Paris, February 1945) looks back from liberation on armistice, schism, épuration and the unfinished task of national reconstruction.

Character of the source. Contemporary political analysis written month by month in London for La France Libre, then gathered and lightly framed for publication in liberated France. Aron writes as “ceux de l’extérieur” who chose Free France early and therefore claim the right to extend the benefit of the doubt where interior résistants could not — while still tracing how attentisme, collaboration and exploitation clarified into national resistance. The tone is diagnostic rather than memoir: signification of facts over anecdotal detail; German occupation as the master frame; Vichy as equivocal, then increasingly police and gangster rule; economic plunder (occupation indemnity, mark rate, orders system, STO) as the constant underneath the ideological comedy of Maurras, Déat, Doriot and Laval. It is a first-rank Free-French exterior chronicle of Occupied and Vichy France from the armistice to the threshold of national insurrection.

Préface (Preface)

The studies collected here appeared in La France Libre in London between November 1940 and May 1944 under the general title “Chroniques de France.” Their object was to help readers — mostly foreign — understand events that daily papers reported confusedly or sensationally. Documentation was inevitably fragmentary; Aron omitted, for example, unverifiable salon anecdotes about Laval’s dismissal (13 December 1940) or recall (April 1942), preferring signification to anecdotal detail.

Writing outside France, he wished to make the whole French reality intelligible. Free France’s own position was known; they had given proofs of their choice, and so felt entitled not to refuse anyone the benefit of the doubt or of mitigating circumstances — not to confuse traitors with the many French of good faith who, for various motives, accepted service under Vichy in 1940. Above all he tried to stay short of, rather than go beyond, the judgments of interior résistants: the risks of the struggle justified their violence; from spring 1941 onward, those of the exterior no longer had that justification.

The collection is not a complete history of the four Occupation years: neither analysis of Resistance movements (security forbade silence-breaking) nor a detailed social map by region (information did not suffice). Interior politics was increasingly shaped by exterior politics, which these pages treat only in general terms. The chronicles bear above all on the German occupation regime of which Vichy’s government, economic exploitation and political collaboration were aspects — and on how, despite enemy ruses and complicities, the French nation ended by uniting against the German. Aron hopes the month-by-month commentaries may still help the French take exact consciousness of what they lived, and prove the effort of “ceux de l’extérieur” to remain in contact with their sufferings, revolts and hopes: never was a people more torn, and never more vivacious and finally irresistible “la volonté des Français d’être une nation.”

Introduction — I. La Capitulation (The Capitulation) — 15 November 1940

Opens on Paul Reynaud’s last words as last Premier of the Third Republic — France will suffer; become fraternal around the wounded patrie; the hour of resurrection will come — and on Marshal Pétain’s radio appeal that combat must be ceased and an armistice sought “entre soldats.” The juxtaposition recalls the whole tragedy: one speech still assigned France a mission in the war; the other concluded that defeat meant stopping the fight. Pétain forgot solemn engagements and the sacred interest binding France to Britain, and forgot that if the métropole was compromised, the Empire, protected by an intact fleet, still held immense resources.

Les acteurs. The armistice request was decided 17 June 1940 at Bordeaux. Aron sets aside salon intrigue and anti-British plotting as illustrations only of the moral decomposition of the Third Republic’s ruling classes. Generals were first to suggest armistice early in June — a reflex, he suggests, akin to German generals in November 1918 preferring political capitulation to too-evident military defeat. Weygand, haunted by fear of revolutionary disorder in collapsing troops, subordinated audacity to social order; political capitulation seemed a way to avoid the open-field military capitulation reproached to the Dutch and the Belgian king.

L’effondrement du régime / La résignation des Français. Under military regime and censorship the government had cut itself off from the nation; it had fought Communists hostile to the war without integrating workers’ organisations into the war union — yet it was in the popular classes that patriotism (resistance to the invader, antifascism) was most vivacious. Mid-June 1940, representatives of the people were sought in vain; under the shock of defeat the Republic collapsed without defending itself. French acceptance of an unparalleled renunciation is explained in two words: défaitisme and malheur — defeatism across parties, demographic fear, desire for quiet, and the revolt against fighting a second war that for twenty years one had sought to avoid rather than win.

Justifications officielles / Politique du moindre mal. Official justifications and the sophism of the “lesser evil” versus continuation of the war are dissected; the chronicle closes that all French history does not fit into summer 1940, that capitulation does not seal the patrie’s destiny, and that meditation on the past must only strengthen lucid action.

Première Partie — Attentisme (Wait-and-See) — Pétain, Darlan/Darnand, Laval

The first part follows Vichy from late 1940 through autumn 1942: the years of attentisme, notables’ rule, economic plunder dressed as collaboration, and the turn toward a police regime after Laval’s return.

II–V. Early Vichy: zones, new regime, misery, notables (Dec. 1940–March 1941)

II. Reconstruction matérielle et atmosphère morale (15 December 1940). Since June, France has been cut off from the free world; a double censorship (weak government + all-powerful invader) leaves foreigners dependent on travellers, tolerated or controlled press. Aron reads both the sold and the enslaved press to guess sentiments and establish facts. France is carved into free zone, occupied zone and forbidden zone (Nord/Est under Brussels Kommandantur); Alsace-Lorraine already integrated into the Reich; the ligne de séparation is an instrument of moral torture and political blackmail with no geographic or military necessity. Material reconstruction, suffering (cold, coal, rationing) and the moral atmosphere of a nation that will not accept defeat as final fill the rest of the chronique.

III. Le nouveau régime — Les hommes et les idées (15 January 1941). For six months the Republic has been called the “régime aboli”; a “révolution nationale” claims unanimous assent while lacking a real governing party. Aron distinguishes among “men of Vichy”: reactionary intellectuals (Alibert, Baudouin, successive education ministers), old Third Republic politicians, fonctionnaires of the abolished regime, military men — and doubts whether Belin or Caziot truly count as “new men.” The catholico-monarchist circle around Pétain dreams of liquidating not only 1789 but of bending history toward a medieval Catholic community; education reform (suppression of écoles normales, compulsory Latin, duties toward God in “lay” schools) follows the Fustel de Coulanges circle. Against this traditionalism stand Paris collaborationists and German pressure: Vichy or Paris, reaction or revolution; the old parties; the emerging politics of collaboration.

IV. Organisation de la misère (15 February 1941). French economic situation is without equivalent: all occupied countries are impoverished, but only France is split into zones with precarious exchange of news and goods, administered jointly by occupation authorities and a government in a spa town, charged with feeding the population and directing industry without powers or raw materials. Vichy’s economic work has a double face — circumstantial response to inexorable constraints, and pretended durable “new economy.” German exploitation begins with the mark fixed at 20 francs — a rate corresponding to official exchange but not to purchasing-power parity — and continues through requisitions, control of coal and iron, and an economy of poverty whose financial problem and immediate “reforms” cannot build a stable order while frontiers and the war’s outcome remain unknown.

V. Le gouvernement des notables (15 March 1941). The men who took power after Reynaud’s resignation differed in origin and aspiration — despairing left parliamentarians, pacifists, fascist sympathisers, traditionalists — but shared one domestic idea: to negotiate with the victor and rebuild, parliamentary rule must yield to authoritarian rule (“Hitler will never treat with a democracy”). Authoritarian form alone does not define the regime; the stakes of struggles around Pétain and between Paris and Vichy are the social-economic-moral content of authority and who will wield it. Parliamentarians fade; high civil and military fonctionnaires, industrial chiefs and grands bourgeois take the upper hand — closer to conservative/traditionalist doctrine than Nazi demagogy, rejecting a single party for fear of revolutionary return, preferring that the masses gradually lose interest in public affairs. The National Revolution is a revolution “from above,” executed by an activist minority, developing with bourgeois collaboration — a return of the notables who once liquidated the Second Empire’s defeat. Liquidation of the abolished regime (moral and material), the new State, and the National Revolution’s content occupy the subsections.

VI–IX. Food, propaganda, culture, collaboration as exploitation (April–July 1941)

VI. Le problème du ravitaillement (15 April 1941). Hitler’s policy toward France, Aron argues from Mein Kampf, aims at France’s destruction — physical (separating men from women, aggravating the birth-rate crisis through privation) and political (envenoming relations with Britain). Food supply must be read inside that plan. From armistice to collaboration, the continent works to feed the German overlord; France’s case is most complex (multiple zones; a government more than half prisoner charged with an economy the occupant elsewhere runs directly). Collaboration presented as voluntary masks what was imposed; the blockade argument and Vichy–Paris polemics over supply serve German strategy.

VII. Propagandes et opinion (15 May 1941; OCR “mas”). In June 1940 the French people had no share in the historic decisions — stunned by disaster, dispersed by evacuations that destroyed the material conditions of public opinion. That passivity is gone: signs multiply, even from Germans and their agents, that the nation has recovered self-consciousness. Radio-Paris attributes Laval’s fall and the slowing of collaboration to “public incomprehension”; Vichy radio tries to disqualify Free France and thereby admits its influence. More than half of France is prisoner, but prisoners do not surrender their souls. German propaganda and Vichy propaganda are analysed against this reawakening; the people whom neither occupant nor Paris/Vichy press can convince that defeat is final contributed to Laval’s fall and will set limits to concessions.

VIII. Culture et société (15 June 1941). Defeat is not only occupation of two-thirds of the territory but the collapse of a society whose supreme values one still refuses to disavow — and distrust of a subtler invasion, of men and ideas foreign to France (Gestapo following tanks; German propaganda depot where students once bought books). Philosophers, poets and novelists recognise that the writer lives in a national community; the task is to save what must be saved of the past and resist those who endanger the authenticity of French culture. An examen de conscience follows the Figaro inquiry to writers (Claudel vs Duhamel on whether pre-war literature went astray); épreuve details destroyed material conditions of culture (occupied Paris, racial declarations for the Salon); menaces et résistance tracks threats and the beginnings of cultural resistance, closing on verse of fidelity to a France “robe sans couture de la foi.”

IX. Collaboration et exploitation : un an après l’armistice (15 July 1941). German economic policy toward France has from day one been dominated by simple ideas: seize transferable wealth (machines, wagons, stocks), then extract the maximum usable for the Reich war machine. Armistice clauses enable indefinite exploitation: the 400-million-franc daily indemnity lets occupants buy available goods; control of coal and iron makes French factories work only when Germans profit. “Collaboration is a subtle form of exploitation, an indirect technique of pillage.” As war lengthens, French industry must restart to meet incompressible needs and produce a surplus — origin of economic collaboration, which also aims politically to rupture France from the Anglo-Saxon democracies and create a fait accompli in “new European” organisation. Subsections treat supply, collaboration and the tribute versus France’s future.

X–XII. From notables to police state; corporation; finances (Sept.–Dec. 1941)

X. Du gouvernement des notables au régime policier (15 September 1941). Since abolition of the Third Republic, two ideological influences work on ruling milieux (not on a country that remains foreign to Vichy): traditionalist doctrines and totalitarian/Nazi doctrines. In foreign policy, attentistes (Maurras’s neutrality; those at Vichy who recoil from armistice extremes) face partisans of integral collaboration (Déat, Doriot). Admiral Darlan, at the height of power, seems to lead the Vichy fraction adopting integral collaboration; attentisme is officially repudiated — yet traditionalist notables remain in press and ministry. Pétain’s 12 June distinction: projects of constitution and laws to rebuild the French social body (Maurrassian doctrine, totalitarian difficulty in practice) versus present realities of mise au pas and collaboration. The slide from notables’ government toward a police regime is the chronique’s arc.

XI. Étatisme et corporation (15 October 1941). Organisation committees and the office of distribution; the Labour Charter — Vichy’s étatist-corporatist machinery as it actually functions under occupation constraint rather than as doctrinal blueprint.

XII. Finances de défaite (15 December 1941; Table: “Finances et défaite”). The circuit of capital; capital market and stock exchanges under occupation — how defeat and tribute reshape French finance, liquidity and the social structure of savings.

XIII–XV. Riom, Laval’s return, military lessons (May–July 1942)

XIII. Défi à Hitler — La France et le procès de Riom (15 May 1942). The opening of the Riom trial (19 February 1942) may mark a new phase: Frenchmen present — not only the accused — refused to obey German will and even to entertain France’s supposed responsibility for the war’s origin. The refusal was public; Hitler took note by attacking France again. A “day of dupes”: the trial meant by Germans to establish French guilt and by Vichy to establish the Republic’s guilt instead gave democracy’s defenders their first tribune in eighteen months. Accused became accusers; disappointed Germans pressed Vichy to end public discussion; the press that had hailed the greatest trial in history degraded it into a “democratic farce.”

XIV. Mise au pas? (15 May 1942; OCR “mat”). Laval’s return to power marks a decisive change in the precarious Vichy regime born of defeat — cut off from the nation’s depths, subject to German blackmail, attacked by enemy agents. New men, ideas and organisations of the collaborationist turn; whether France can be fully mise au pas remains undecided — Laval in 1942 is not Hitler in 1933; he has no support but those who staked existence on German victory, and French politics remains subordinate to battles across the planet.

XV. Défaites d’hier, leçons actuelles — Le problème militaire au procès de Riom (15 July 1942). By suspending Riom sine die, Vichy confessed the failure of the enterprise meant to discredit the Republic forever; Barthélemy’s formula about campaigns compromising “international relations” discreetly alludes to Hitler’s anger that Frenchmen had publicly named Germany alone responsible for the war. Beyond politics, military data from interrogations deserve study: material deficits, insufficiency and delay, strategic and tactical errors — all essential questions at least posed at Riom. Closing refrain: the French soldier did not demerit; France will find again the road of greatness.

XVI–XVIII. Ideological comedy and legalised pillage (Aug.–Oct. 1942)

XVI. La comédie politique (15 August 1942). For a century and a half France lived modern ideological conflicts with unique intensity; since the armistice old quarrels are backgrounded because the decisive line runs between the immense majority against the invader and the weak minority supporting Vichy or total collaboration — yet that minority splits into rival “right” and “left,” Maurras vituperating democracy in Action Française, Déat reaction in L’Œuvre. Discussions aim at camouflage and self-justification. Since Laval’s return, roles reverse: traditionalists were in power with Déat/Doriot as left opposition; now European-socialism collaborationists hold power and traditionalists play opposition — “from integral nationalism to European socialism.”

XVII. Légalisation du pillage (15 September 1942). Liquidation of French assets abroad; German participation in French firms; the great agreements of 1940–1942 (Francolor and others) — plunder poured into legal forms so that neither efficacy of domination nor appearance of law is lost.

XVIII. Nouvelle technique d’exploitation (15 October 1942). Exploitation remains the constant while German armies dominate the continent; modalities change with German needs, French resources and military events. Exhaustion of French raw-material reserves, German manpower needs after Eastern losses, and the chance of a second front drive a shift: the orders system, industrial concentration, and labour conscription — prelude to the deportations that Partie II will place at the centre of the aggravated ordeal.

Deuxième Partie — Collaboration — Pétain, Laval, Darnand

After November 1942 — empire back in the war, fleet scuttled at Toulon, all France occupied, full powers effectively with Laval — attentisme loses its remaining props. Partie II tracks disintegration, famine and deportation, the end of ambiguities, and the gangster-police climax on the eve of the second battle of France.

XIX–XXII. After November 1942: regime, food, abstention, myths (Dec. 1942–March 1943)

XIX. La désagrégation du régime de Vichy (15 January 1943). The name Vichy will remain tied to the most tragic period of French history. For two and a half years Frenchmen suffered occupation’s rigours and the moral tearing of an almost indecipherable situation — a government not assimilable to Quisling regimes by composition and intentions, yet acting against national aspirations. The equivoke rested on metropolitan impotence combined with remaining assets (fleet and empire) that left a narrow margin of autonomous decision; occupants tried to drag Vichy into military collaboration; Vichy used its assets to limit German violence and reserve the future. In November 1942 that game ended. Subsections: two and a half years of the provisional armistice regime; empire and fleet.

XX. Problèmes du ravitaillement (15 December 1942). Progressive aggravation of food supply: lack of fertiliser and machines cuts production; lack of transport prevents rational distribution; occupation authorities increase levies as Germany feels the blockade. Military events (loss of North African imports) seriously modify the calculus — harvests, transport crisis.

XXI. Collaboration ou abstention? (15 February 1943). November 1942 clarified ranks without increasing cohesion among National Revolution tenants. Déat and Doriot continue polemics over the promised single party; both oppose the Légion. Maurras’s jargon of the old right and Déat’s of the old left still face off — counter-revolution versus European socialist revolution, “seule France” versus “seule Europe” — even though attentisme and collaborationism have lost their former meaning: what remains to wait for, for a government without army, navy, colonies or unoccupied zone? Behind the dialectic lie two moral attitudes more than political programmes; the chronique weighs retrospective polemic and projects for the future.

XXII. Empire de Charlemagne et testament de Richelieu (15 March 1943). The French empire’s return to war is, for Hitler’s Germany, a diplomatic defeat comparable to Stalingrad militarily: against France as against no other country Hitler had deployed corruption, ruse and propaganda; in November 1942 the veil tore. Why did Germans prize the collaboration formula? Early on, to have the new order recognised by Anglo-Saxons and accepted by the defeated (Megerle in the Börsenzeitung at Montoire: if France freely said yes, England would lose its continental ally and American interventionists their strongest psychological arguments). Theory and practice of the Charlemagne-empire myth versus the Richelieu testament of French state interest structure the rest.

XXIII–XXV. Economic chaos, aggravated ordeal, national impoverishment (May–Aug. 1943)

XXIII. Le chaos économique (15 May 1943). Industrial order and disorder; tribute and inflation — the economic counterpart of political disintegration.

XXIV. Épreuve aggravée (15 June 1943). At the threshold of summer 1943, while French armies take a glorious part in African victory, the nation on home soil struggles for existence and liberty. Deportations and hunger worsen; against them French need no less courage than against the enemy. Since November 1942 ambiguities have dissipated; sordid intrigues of Laval, Déat, Doriot over scraps of illusory authority unfold on the margin of a country united against the invader. Maurras still defends non-belligerence with desperate dialectic; growing occupation rigour and national fervour liquidate intermediate positions. The débâcle of collaboration is consumed; France lives in expectation of liberation. Subsections: deportations; dearth and German levies; débâcle of the collaborators.

XXV. Appauvrissement de la nation (15 August 1943). German pillage and French poverty; misery of the masses — the cumulative balance-sheet of three years of organised extraction.

XXVI–XXIX. End of ambiguities to the second battle of France (Oct. 1943–May 1944)

XXVI. La fin des équivoques (15 October 1943). The press of both theoretical zones gives an impression of monotony or paralysis — Maurras endlessly repeating strict obedience, Déat the European Revolution — yet even crystallised thought cannot hide change: ambiguities have disappeared; on one side the clique of traitors, voluntary or involuntary prisoners of their treason; on the other a unanimous nation. Simplification tracks military operations; not all rallies to Resistance are disinterested — yet it would be unjust to see only opportunism. The properly “Vichyist” clientele, recruited especially among traditionally conservative Catholic bourgeoisie, led astray by a Marshal’s prestige, “seule France” sophisms and traditionalist counter-revolution illusions, was brought back not only by Allied victories and the empire’s return to war but by occupation methods themselves; the Catholic Church’s attitude was both witness and expression of that change. From Pétainisme to Resistance; from the Légion to the Milice.

XXVII. Dernières illusions (15 January 1944). Since 1940 French politics unfolds simultaneously inside and outside the occupied country. Until November 1942 interior events weighed more because most of the empire and the fleet answered to Vichy; since then the relation reversed — stripped of material force and of Pétain’s former prestige, Vichy would degrade into a mere instrument of the enemy if most fonctionnaires did not sabotage orders from patriotism. The near-indifference surrounding Vichy men and things, in France and abroad, is shown by the cool reception of the 13 November 1943 quarrel between Pétain and the Germans — a year earlier it would have been read as late rupture; now it is only an episode in the history of a vanishing state. German terror; the phantom of a return to the National Assembly.

XXVIII. Les gangsters au pouvoir (15 March 1944). Promotion to minister of a common-law criminal such as Joseph Darnand inaugurates a new stage of the French calvary and completes a triple evolution since 1940: the diverse elements that accepted Pétain’s symbolic authority and National Revolution formula have dissociated — most joined Resistance; by end-1942 attentistes had deserted Vichy in mass; by end-1943 the last attentistes were eliminated from government and the representative of Paris collaborationists, sold body and soul to the enemy, is charged with “maintaining order,” i.e. hunting patriots. Parallel, revolt against enemy and accomplices gained amplitude, violence, organisation, unity. France’s tragedy: a few tens of thousands of irreducible collaborators remain, thanks to German presence, masters of the State and thus use thousands of gendarmes and police who are mostly good Frenchmen. Occupation policy itself shifted from Wehrmacht order-and-exploitation plus Abetz diplomatic seduction plus Gestapo terror toward Gestapo predominance; Darnand reveals the regime’s secret. The last Vichy government.

XXIX. Vers la deuxième bataille de France (15 May 1944). Month by month the chronicles should leave political analysis for war communiqués: sabotages, clashes between patriots and miliciens, attacks on the occupation army multiply; Germans and French alike act on military considerations — Wehrmacht security before and on landing day; French harassing the enemy while accumulating forces for the decisive battle. Official Vichy policy, insofar as it still exists, merely translates Hitlerite staff intentions: Déat recruits slaves as Darnand executes Gestapo dirty work; Henriot puts Goebbels’s threats into French — all part of one enterprise whose supreme aim, for Nazis at bay, is to create chaos. Portraits of Déat, Henriot and Darnand close the wartime series on the threshold of the second battle of France.

Note finale (Final Note) — Paris, February 1945

Written from liberated Paris, the note looks back on armistice choices with strategic counterfactuals that Aron admits must remain uncertain: had the empire resisted in 1940, would Germany still have attacked Russia in June 1941 with Allies mastering the Mediterranean? The unreal is only more or less problematic reconstruction; yet the condition of the Hitlerite enterprise eastward seems to have been relative security in the west — the June armistice, a sort of desperate reply to the German–Soviet pact, probably hastened Russian intervention without which exhaustion of the Wehrmacht would have been impossible. These remarks do not alter the judgment due at the moment of decision: France still had some thousand first-line aircraft and a fleet superior to Italy’s; withdrawing them overnight increased chances of a German lightning victory. Men fiercely resolved to liberate the patrie would not have taken that risk lightly — but Marquet and Laval thought of liquidating the war, not winning it. The armistice was neither conceived nor presented as a mere military expedient; French prestige abroad was hit harder than homebound French could imagine.

On the “schism of Vichy”: resolute collaborationists were always a weak minority that excluded itself from the nation; what reappeared was the traditional French schism in a new figure — Maurras’s “revenge of the Dreyfusards” would be grotesque had early traditionalist Vichy not been, to a point, the revenge of the anti-Dreyfusards. The melancholy impression is that things changed less than one had hoped: some conservatives infected by German poisons and Vichy’s equivoke on national duty; others forced closer to the mass of French in combat — yet all told, French bourgeoisie, business milieux and bien-pensants remained strangely like themselves. If one wishes to spare the country a bloody revolution, one asks the bourgeoisie to become again a true elite — clairvoyant about reforms, not sullen toward popular aspirations.

Armistice, Montoire, Vichy, collaboration would vanish from memory as accidental episodes if the present did not give them sense — if representatives of contradictory attitudes did not survive, impatient for other occasions to clash. One must first overcome the four tragic years — eliminate the guilty, bring the weak, conformists and misguided back to the French community — but above all overcome the nation’s traditional divisions. Yesterday’s quarrels weigh heavily; had épuration been better conducted, punishing principal responsibles quickly and solemnly, it would have been easier to disregard small fry and avoid one part of opinion protesting excess and the other insufficiency. Leave regrets: the task’s difficulty inclines the critic of good faith to indulgence. It is no longer a matter of looking backward. The French will not change on command; one must rebuild France with the French as they are. Hope is not to tear them suddenly from themselves but to unite them in a common task. National insurrection marked the triumphant completion of a work pursued four years in the night; today one must build in full daylight, without hope of dazzling days or perspective of triumph — an indefinite task requiring effort each day.
