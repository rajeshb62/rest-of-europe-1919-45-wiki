Adolf Hitler: Das Zeitalter der Verantwortungslosigkeit. Eine Biographie

Konrad Heiden — Adolf Hitler: Das Zeitalter der Verantwortungslosigkeit. Eine Biographie (Adolf Hitler: The Age of Irresponsibility. A Biography). Europa-Verlag, Zürich, 1936. The first full-length biography of Hitler, written in exile by the German-Jewish journalist and critic of National Socialism, from Hitler’s birth and youth in Austria to the Night of the Long Knives of 30 June 1934.

AuthorKonrad Heiden (1901–1966) — German-Jewish journalist and political writer; as a reporter on the Bavarian political scene he attended National Socialist meetings from 1922 and followed the movement from within; author of the earlier Geschichte des Nationalsozialismus (1932) and Geburt des Dritten Reiches (1934); emigrated in 1933 and wrote this biography from Switzerland
Edition1936, Europa-Verlag G. m. b. H., Zürich; printed in Switzerland (Druckerei-Genossenschaft Aarau); preface dated Zürich, 20 August 1935; a volume of the publisher’s exile list, issued in the third year of the regime it chronicles
StructurePreface + 20 chapters in 3 parts; 19,465 lines / ~968,000 characters of extracted text
Period1889–1934: from Hitler’s birth at Braunau am Inn and his youth in Linz and Vienna, through the First World War, the founding of the NSDAP and the Beer Hall Putsch, the years of reorganisation and electoral advance, to the seizure of power in 1933 and the purge of 30 June 1934
NoteThe book’s subtitle, “Das Zeitalter der Verantwortungslosigkeit” (the age of irresponsibility), supplies its thesis: that Hitler’s rise was the product of an epoch in which the German middle classes abandoned responsibility, and that the biography is at once a portrait of the man and an explanation of “this present-day Germany,” in Heiden’s words from the preface; based on fifteen years of study, observation from close range, and the use of confidential informants some of whom remained active near Hitler

Contents. A critical biography of Hitler, from his birth and formative years to the consolidation of the dictatorship. The three parts trace his Austrian childhood and the “lost years” in Vienna and Munich, his war service and the discovery of his political vocation; the founding and early growth of the National Socialist movement, its propaganda and organisation, the Beer Hall Putsch of 1923, the imprisonment and writing of Mein Kampf, and the seven years of reconstruction and electoral advance after 1925; and finally the Hitler of power — his personality and private life, his entourage, the Reichstag fire, and the massacre of 30 June 1934, with which the book closes. Heiden presents Hitler less as a master of events than as the creation of his age, the “great inferior one” who rose because the responsible classes failed.

Character of the source. The principal non-Nazi biographical portrait of Hitler written during the Third Reich, by a contemporary who had observed the movement from inside since 1922. Heiden’s standpoint is that of the independent democratic opponent of National Socialism: he neither heroises nor demonises, describing Hitler as “neither a superman nor a bogeyman, but a very interesting contemporary and, numerically considered, the greatest mass-agitator in world history.” Written in exile and published in Switzerland, it draws on observation from close range and on confidential informants, several of whom remained in the Führer’s entourage. For this wiki it supplies the German case from the critical-democratic side — the counterpart to the regime’s own documentary compilations (the Dokumente der deutschen Politik volumes) and to the völkisch racial tract of Geisow, all of which treat the same rise from within the National Socialist camp.

Vorwort (Preface)

Dated Zürich, 20 August 1935. States the book’s genesis in the need “to say what is,” impossible in Germany where the interests of the state stand against objective research; describes the basis of the work in fifteen years of study, observation from close range, and the use of all accessible sources open and confidential, including informants still active near Hitler; notes that objectivity is not freedom from standpoint — the hero is neither a superman nor a bogeyman but “a very interesting contemporary and, numerically considered, the greatest mass-agitator of world history”; closes with the epigraph from Goethe’s Dichtung und Wahrheit on the demonic.

Erster Teil (First Part)

1. Kapitel: Heimat und Herkunft (Home and Origins)

Hitler’s Austrian birth and childhood. The “Schicklgruber-Hitler” family, the failed examination and the early failure in Linz; the years in Vienna — the encounter with the working class, the Jews, and Karl Lueger the anti-Semitic mayor of Vienna as the model; the departure from Vienna for Munich.

2. Kapitel: Der Krieg als Erlöser (The War as Redeemer)

The First World War as Hitler’s deliverance from an aimless life: his gratitude for the war, the Iron Cross, the unrecognised leader, the front as home; Heiden’s account of “Hitler’s world-historical error” and the failure of Pasewalk (the alleged discovery of his political mission in the field hospital).

Zweiter Teil (Second Part)

3. Kapitel: Umkehr in der Sackgasse (The Turning in the Cul-de-sac)

The failed revolutionary and the “strike of the individual” in postwar Munich: a useless talent, the stairway into nothingness, the devaluation of the parties, illusions against interests, the failure of the revolution of 1918–19.

4. Kapitel: Der Aufbruch (The Departure)

The beginnings of the movement: Dietrich Eckart in search of a leader, the “iron fist” of Röhm, and the early slogan of “breaking the bondage of interest” (Brechung der Zinsknechtschaft).

5. Kapitel: Der Klassenkampf der Intellektuellen (The Class Struggle of the Intellectuals)

Hitler’s obscure vocation and the “illusion-bourgeois”; “Jewish Marxism”; the development of anti-Semitism from a social game into a mass madness; the Pan-Germans and the Fatherland Party; “the privilege of race”; member No. 7, Karl Harrer, Dietrich Eckart’s advice, and the program assembly.

6. Kapitel: Propaganda und Organisation (Propaganda and Organisation)

The instruments of the movement’s growth: wit, logic and insolence; the refuted theories; the symbols; the listener as collaborator; the art of oratory; “Hitler always says the same thing…”; the Führerprinzip (leadership principle).

7. Kapitel: Der Weg in die Gesellschaft (The Way into Society)

How the movement entered respectable society: the question of Hitler’s livelihood, “in the circle of beautiful women,” the struggle for the salons, at tea and biscuits — the courtship of the upper middle classes.

8. Kapitel: Stufen zur Macht (Steps to Power)

The Bavarian fronde and the Kapp Putsch; the tireless organiser; the emergence of “the Führer”; the beginnings of the SA, terror, the Black Reichswehr; word of honour and the consecration of flags; the break with the Reichswehr.

9. Kapitel: Der Putsch (The Putsch)

The Beer Hall Putsch of November 1923: the General State Commissioner von Kahr against Berlin, Lossow’s 51 percent, Hitler’s panic at the closing door; the shot in the Bürgerbräukeller, the march to the Feldherrnhalle, the collapse, Hitler’s flight — and the question “was Hitler permitted to flee?“

10. Kapitel: Die Rückkehr zum Herrn Präsidenten (The Return to the Lord President)

The aftermath of the putsch: an attempted suicide, the judicial comedy of the trial, Hitler and Dimitroff, the separation from Ludendorff, Gürtner’s rescue of Hitler, behind comfortable walls in Landsberg; the dissolution of the movement; the writing of Mein Kampf; the first brown shirt, the release, “peace with Rome,” the whip; the refounding of the party, the first break with Röhm, and the appearance of Hindenburg.

11. Kapitel: Sieben Jahre auf dem Zauberberg (Seven Years on the Magic Mountain)

The years of reconstruction 1925–1932, named for Thomas Mann’s novel: the writer in Haus Wachenfeld, the Straßer–Goebbels rivalry, riots and trials, Goebbels’ desertion of Straßer, the blood flag and the advertisements, the homosexuals, Heines’ expulsion, the terror of the offices, the lawsuit over Prince Rupprecht, idyll and intrigue, and how the bourgeoisie capitulated.

12. Kapitel: Der große Minderwertige (The Great Inferior One)

Heiden’s analysis of Hitler’s character and social base: from the life of a good-for-nothing; collapsing classes, the desecration of property, the reactionary revolution, privilege and irresponsibility, the defection of all classes; “Hitler’s ripened field.”

13. Kapitel: Die diplomatische Periode (The Diplomatic Period)

The movement’s courtship of power and money: Göring’s return, Otto Dietrich’s mission, foreign money, heavy industry, the incapable reaction, “the magnet before the dungheap,” the break with Otto Strasser, Pfeffer’s fall, the “military-political association,” courting the Reichswehr, and the 107 parliamentary seats.

14. Kapitel: Der Einbruch in den Staat (The Break-in into the State)

The assault on the republic: Röhm’s return, “the Bohemian corporal,” the “System!,” disunited opponents, the sham struggle against Hindenburg, the reserve army of the Junkers, and how Röhm overthrew Brüning.

15. Kapitel: Der Wettlauf mit der Katastrophe (The Race with the Catastrophe)

The final crisis of the republic: the decay of Social Democracy, the mistakes of the Communists, Severing yielding to force, the blow with the walking-stick, the descent; in Schleicher’s pincers, Gregor Strasser’s last struggle, a weary proletariat, a weary capitalism, the Cologne alliance, mines and countermines, Schleicher’s fall — “at the goal,” the transfer of power to Hitler in January 1933.

Dritter Teil (Third Part)

16. Kapitel: Die beiden Hitler (The Two Hitlers)

The Hitler of power: egoist or altruist, the “wonderful double nature,” the mask, “le style c’est l’homme,” his relation to the intellect, inclinations and habits, the road upward, the inner voice.

17. Kapitel: Die Frauen (The Women)

Hitler and women: an opaque eroticism, a puritanical man, a failed love, “Alf and Geli” (the suicide of Geli Raubal), “Adolf Hitler’s secret.”

18. Kapitel: Die Begleitfiguren (The Companion Figures)

The entourage: the vanished founders, two accomplices, the confessor, the ornament of the movement, the powerless cleverness, a Caesar without ambition, Cheka and anti-Cheka, a disappointment, subservient youth, Julius Streicher “the true Hitler,” the Pettenkofer system.

19. Kapitel: Am schwankenden Steuer (At the Shaky Helm)

The first months of power: the burning of the Reichstag, the atrocities, the licence to kill, “the streets of Adolf Hitler.”

20. Kapitel: Der 30. Juni (The 30th of June)

The Night of the Long Knives: an unplanned dictatorship, Röhm’s failed advances, the Marburg speech, Schleicher against “the criminals,” Röhm’s encirclement, the corporal and the generals, on a knife’s edge, Hitler contradicting himself; with armoured cars and pistol, shooting at homosexuals; legend and reality, at the wall, Göring and the prince, the bloodbath, “end with terror.” The book closes with the account of Dr. Glaser of Munich, shot by three SS men on the evening of 30 June, and the urn of ashes handed to his brother — “the ashes of the unknown German who fell victim to the highest responsibility assumed on 30 June.”
