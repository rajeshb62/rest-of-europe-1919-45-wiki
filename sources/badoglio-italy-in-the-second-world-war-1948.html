Badoglio — Italy in the Second World War: Memories and Documents (1948)

Author: Marshal Pietro Badoglio (1871–1956)

Publisher: Oxford University Press, London, 1948 (English translation by Muriel Currey of L’Italia nella seconda guerra mondiale); reprint by Greenwood Press, Westport, Connecticut, 1976. Library of Congress catalog card 75-28658; ISBN 0-8371-8485-1. (Digitized copy from the Baker College–Flint library, via Internet Archive, oclc 61198029.)

Preface: dated “Cava dei Tirreni, July 1944” — “I have written this book because I believe that the Italian people have the right to know the course of events which led to their ruin. I do not wish to try to justify myself. Only the man who acts makes mistakes.”

Character

The memoir of Marshal Pietro Badoglio, the Army Chief of Staff under the Fascist regime who led the Italian conquest of Ethiopia and commanded in the Greek and North African theatres, and who in July 1943 became Prime Minister after the King’s coup against Mussolini. Written at Cava dei Tirreni in the summer of 1944 — between the liberation of Rome and the end of the war — it is at once an apologia for Badoglio’s own conduct and a denunciation of the Fascist political and military leadership that he held responsible for Italy’s ruin. It is the testament of the man who dismantled the Fascist state from within, negotiated the armistice with the Allies, and presided over the cobelligerent government at Brindisi, Salerno and Rome. Where the other Italian sources in this wiki are regime-side narratives of Fascism’s rise, this is the Allied-side account of Fascism’s fall and of Italy’s wartime ordeal, told by the regime’s own military commander who turned against it.

Structure

Two parts, eighteen chapters (numbered I–VI, VIII–XIX; the number VII is skipped in the text), followed by six appendix documents. Part I covers the road to war and the fall of the regime (1939 – 8 September 1943); Part II covers the armistice, the Brindisi government and the cobelligerent period (September 1943 – June 1944).

  • Part I (chs. I–VI, VIII–IX) — 1939 to the Armistice

    • Ch. I (1939) — Mussolini’s memorandum of April 1939 to Hitler (the “rich vs. poor nations” war thesis); the character of Mussolini and Hitler
    • Ch. II — Mussolini decides on Intervention — the Polish campaign, the decision of 10 June 1940 and Italy’s declaration of war
    • Ch. III — At War — the command of the armed forces, the campaign against France and the early war years
    • Ch. IV — The Dictatorship ‘in extremis’ — Badoglio’s resignation as Chief of the General Staff, the war disasters and the crisis of the regime
    • Ch. V — The ‘Coup d’état’ — the conspiracy, the meeting of the Grand Council (24–25 July 1943), the arrest of Mussolini and the King’s appointment of Badoglio
    • Ch. VI — A Review of the Situation — the reasons for continuing the war, negotiations with the Allies
    • Ch. VIII — The Armistice — the negotiations with the Allies and the armistice of 8 September 1943 (the number VII is not used)
    • Ch. IX — From Rome to Brindisi — the flight of the King, Badoglio and the government from Rome to the Allied lines at Brindisi

  • Part II (chs. X–XIX) — the Cobelligerent Government, September 1943 – June 1944

    • Ch. X — First Days at Brindisi
    • Ch. XI — Military Problems — the Anglo-American strategy in Italy, the co-belligerent Italian armed forces
    • Ch. XII — The Difficulties Increase
    • Ch. XIII — The Government extends its Jurisdiction — the transfer to Salerno, the re-establishment of administration
    • Ch. XIV — The Pact with Russia: Roosevelt’s Declaration — the Soviet recognition of the Italian government
    • Ch. XV — The New Provinces: The Allied Control Commission: The Elimination of Fascism
    • Ch. XVI — The Constitutional Compromise — the debate over the King’s position and the Crown
    • Ch. XVII — The Government of Democratic Unity — the formation of the six-party government under the anti-Fascist parties
    • Ch. XVIII — The Liberation of Rome: The Lieutenant-Generalship: The New Government — the fall of Rome (June 1944), the Prince’s lieutenant-generalship, Badoglio’s resignation
    • Ch. XIX — Italy and the Allies — a summing-up of the cobelligerence

  • Appendixes — six documents

    • Document 1 — The Royal Italian Army (its war record and reorganisation)
    • Document 2 — The Royal Italian Navy
    • Document 3 — The Royal Italian Air Force
    • Document 4 — The Government and the Patriots — the guerrilla/partisan movement and the decision to support it
    • Document 5 — The Prisoners of War
    • Document 6 — Internal Reconstruction

Coverage

The Second World War as experienced by Italy’s wartime government, from April 1939 (Mussolini’s memorandum to Hitler) through Italy’s declaration of war in June 1940, the campaigns in France, Greece, North Africa and the Soviet Union, the fall of Mussolini and the coup of 25 July 1943, the armistice of 8 September 1943, the German occupation and the establishment of the cobelligerent government at Brindisi and Salerno, to the liberation of Rome in June 1944. It is the counterpart to, and refutation of, the Fascist-side accounts of the rise of the regime: where Businelli, Volpe, Farinacci and Pini narrate Fascism’s victory, Badoglio narrates its collapse and Italy’s passage to the Allied side.

I–III (1937–39); Pini & Bresadola, Storia del Fascismo (1928). Badoglio stands at the opposite pole of the same political history — the account of the regime’s military commander who overthrew it.
  • Companion German memoir: Speer, Erinnerungen (1969) — the analogous insider’s memoir of a defeated dictatorship written after its fall.
