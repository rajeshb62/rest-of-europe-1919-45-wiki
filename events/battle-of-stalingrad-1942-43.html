The Battle of Stalingrad (19 November 1942 – 2 February 1943)

19 November 1942 – 2 February 1943

The battle of Stalingrad was the decisive battle of the Soviet-German front, in which the Red Army, halting the German advance at the walls of the city on the Volga in the autumn of 1942, passed to the counter-offensive on 19 November and, encircling and destroying an enormous picked German army, seized the initiative of the war and began the mass expulsion of the enemy from the Soviet land. In the official account, set down in the orders of the People’s Commissar of Defence and Supreme Commander Stalin, the battle at Stalingrad was the greatest battle in the history of wars — the turning point at which the German-fascist army met its sunset and the Soviet people saw the beginning of their victory. The order of 7 November 1942, on the twenty-fifth anniversary of October, announced the transition of the initiative to the Red Army, and the order of 28 February 1943, on the twenty-fifth anniversary of the Red Army, recorded the destruction of the German forces at Stalingrad and the beginning of the mass expulsion of the enemy.

The summer crisis of 1942

The summer of 1942 had brought the deepest crisis of the war. The order of 7 November 1942 recalled how the Red Army, in the winter period, had beaten off the German attack on Moscow, taken the initiative into its hands and driven the German troops to the west, liberating a whole series of regions of the country. In the summer, however, the position on the front changed for the worse. Taking advantage of the absence of a second front in Europe, the Germans and their allies swept together all their reserves and threw them onto the Soviet front, breaking it at heavy cost and advancing in the south, placing Stalingrad, the Black Sea coast, Grozny and the approaches to the Transcaucasus under threat. The plan of the German command, as the order explained, had been to pass round Moscow from the east, cut it off from the Volga and Ural rear and then strike at the capital; the advance to the south, toward the oil regions, had the auxiliary purpose of drawing off the principal Soviet reserves and weakening the Moscow front. The firmness and courage of the Red Army frustrated the plan for the outflanking of Moscow. The enemy was halted at Stalingrad; but, halted there and already laying down tens of thousands of his soldiers and officers, he threw new divisions into battle, straining his last forces, and the struggle on the Soviet-German front grew ever more intense. On the outcome of this struggle, the order declared, depended the fate of the Soviet state, the freedom and independence of the Motherland.

The enemy halted at Stalingrad

In this decisive moment, the order of 7 November 1942 was addressed to the army and the people on the twenty-fifth anniversary of the Great October Socialist Revolution, celebrated “in the full swing of the cruel struggle against the German-fascist invaders.” The order drew the balance of the first year of the war: the calculations of the German imperialists on the disintegration of the Soviet state had failed completely; socialist industry, the collective-farm order, the friendship of the peoples of the country and the Soviet state itself had shown their firmness and indestructibility, and the whole rear worked selflessly for the needs of the front. The Red Army, bearing the whole weight of the war against Hitler’s Germany and its accomplices, had learned to beat the enemy for certain; the heroic defenders of Moscow and Tula, of Odessa and Sevastopol, of Leningrad and Stalingrad had shown examples of selfless bravery, iron discipline, firmness and the ability to conquer. In the course of the war the Red Army had put out of action over eight million enemy soldiers and officers, and the Hitlerite army, diluted with Rumanians, Hungarians, Italians and Finns, had become far weaker than in the summer and autumn of 1941. The order set the immediate tasks — to defend the line of the front steadfastly, to exhaust the enemy and destroy his men and material, to strengthen iron discipline and unity of command, and to prepare a shattering blow — and closed with the certainty that the day was not far off when the enemy would know the force of new blows of the Red Army. “There will be a holiday on our street as well,” ran the celebrated closing.

The counter-offensive of 19 November

The counter-offensive at Stalingrad opened on 19 November 1942. Three months after the order of 7 November, the order of 28 February 1943 recorded that the troops of the Red Army had begun the offensive on the approaches to Stalingrad, and that since then the initiative of military operations had been in their hands, while the tempo and striking power of the offensives did not slacken. Advancing in the severe conditions of winter along a front of fifteen hundred kilometres, the Red Army attacked almost everywhere with success — at Leningrad, on the Central front, on the approaches to Kharkov, in the Donbas, at Rostov and on the shores of the Azov and Black seas. In three months it liberated from the enemy the territory of the Voronezh and Stalingrad regions, the Chechen-Ingush, North-Ossetian, Kabardino-Balkarian and Kalmyk autonomous republics, the Stavropol and Krasnodar territories, and almost the whole of the Rostov, Kharkov and Kursk regions. “The mass expulsion of the enemy from the Soviet country has begun,” the order declared, and it asked whence such serious failures of the Germans had come and what had changed in these three months — the question answered by the record of the encirclement and destruction of the German forces at Stalingrad.

The destruction of the encircled army

The encirclement and liquidation of the German forces at Stalingrad was the decisive result of the counter-offensive. The order of 28 February 1943 recorded the encircling and liquidation of an enormous picked German army of three hundred and thirty thousand men at Stalingrad, the capture of over three hundred thousand enemy soldiers and officers in the winter campaign, and the destruction of a vast quantity of enemy men and material; only a correct strategy of the command of the Red Army and the flexible tactics of its commanders, the official verdict insisted, could have brought about so outstanding a fact as the encirclement and liquidation of so large an army. The order of 7 November 1943, surveying the completed year, fixed the scale of the battle: the battle of Stalingrad had ended with the encirclement of the three-hundred-thousand-strong German army, its rout and the capture of about a third of the encircled troops; to give an idea of the unprecedented carnage that had played itself out on the fields of Stalingrad, it recorded that at the end of the battle 147,200 killed German soldiers and officers and 46,700 killed Soviet soldiers and officers had been gathered up and buried. “Stalingrad was the sunset of the German-fascist army,” the order concluded; after the Stalingrad massacre, the Germans could no longer recover. By the beginning of February 1943 the liquidation of the encircled army was complete, and the city on the Volga, held through the summer and autumn, had become the symbol of the turning of the war.

The turning of the war

The battle of Stalingrad was the turning point of the Great Patriotic War. From Stalingrad the Red Army never again let the initiative out of its hands; its blows throughout the following summer became ever stronger and its military skill increased with every month. The order of 7 November 1943 fixed the verdict that, from a purely military point of view, the defeat of the German forces by the end of that year had been predetermined by two most important events — the battle of Stalingrad and the battle of Kursk. The attempt of the Hitlerite command to take revenge for Stalingrad by a counter-offensive in the region of Kharkov, designed to arrange a “German Stalingrad” for the Soviet troops, failed; and the winter campaign of 1942–1943 showed that the offensive power of the Red Army had grown. In the official account the battle at the walls of the Volga city stood as the greatest battle in the history of wars — the battle in which the German-fascist army, halted, encircled and destroyed, met its sunset and in which the Soviet people first saw the coming victory of their just war.
