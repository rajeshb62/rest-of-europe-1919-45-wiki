The Collectivisation of Agriculture (1929–1934)

1929–1934

The collectivisation of agriculture was the campaign by which the Communist Party and the Soviet government brought the scattered individual holdings of the village into collective farms — the колхоз (kolkhoz) — uniting the land, the livestock and the implements of the peasants in the common economy of the producers. It was the sequel to the socialist industrialisation of the Five-Year Plans, and it rested on the decision, proclaimed by the party in 1929, to abandon the earlier policy of restricting the kulaks and to pass to the liquidation of the kulaks as a class (раскулачивание). Carried through in the years 1929–1934, the campaign transformed the countryside: whole villages and districts entered the kolkhozes, the tractors of the machine-tractor stations came to the fields, and the socialist system of agriculture was established alongside the socialist industry of the towns. The party’s own account of these years — the Short Course of party history, the biography of Stalin and the school textbook of Shestakov — presents the collectivisation as a necessary and victorious stage of the construction of socialism, undertaken against the resistance of the kulak and of the oppositionists who had defended him.

The course of the Fifteenth Congress

The Fifteenth Congress of the party, meeting in December 1927, had adopted the course on the collectivisation of agriculture. Socialist industrialisation had advanced, but agriculture lagged behind: the small, scattered individual holdings could not supply the growing towns and industrial centres, and the kulak — the prosperous peasant who hired labour and held back the surplus grain — stood in the way of the socialist supply of the country. The congress resolved on the all-round extension of the collectivisation of agriculture and laid down a plan for the expansion and strengthening of the network of kolkhozes and совхозов (state farms). The growth of socialist industry, which had begun the mass production of tractors and machines for agriculture, supplied the material base; the decisive struggle against the kulaks during the grain-collection campaigns of 1928 and 1929, the growth of agricultural co-operation, and the good experience of the first kolkhozes and sovkhozes together prepared the transition to the collective economy. The peasants, broken into petty and scattered individual holdings, without tolerable implements and draught power, and ground down by poverty and isolation, found in the union of the small farms into collectives, in the tractor and in the help of the state, the road to a better life and the liberation from the kulak yoke.

The turn to the liquidation of the kulaks

Until the end of 1929 the Soviet government had conducted a policy of restricting the kulak elements — taxing the kulak, requiring the sale of his grain to the state at fixed prices, and limiting the size of the kulak holding by the laws on land rent and hired labour — but not of liquidating the kulaks as a class. The kulaks answered the advance of the party with resistance: they refused in masses to sell their surplus grain to the Soviet state, conducted terror against the kolkhoz members and the party and Soviet workers in the villages, and set fire to the kolkhozes and the state grain-collection points. Stalin, addressing the conference of agrarian-Marxists in December 1929, laid down the new policy: with the growth of the kolkhozes and sovkhozes, which could now replace the kulak grain production by their own, the party could pass from the policy of restricting the kulak elements to the policy of liquidating the kulaks as a class on the basis of wholesale collectivisation. At the end of 1929 the Soviet government made the sharp turn: it abolished the laws on land rent and on hired labour, thus depriving the kulak of both land and hired workers; it removed the prohibition on razkulachivanie; and it permitted the peasants to confiscate the cattle, the machines and the implements of the kulaks in favour of the kolkhozes. The kulak class was expropriated as the capitalists had been expropriated in 1918, with the difference that the means of production of the kulaks passed this time not into the hands of the state but into the hands of the united peasants, of the kolkhozes.

The policy was fixed in the historic decision of the Central Committee of 5 January 1930, “On the rate of collectivisation and the measures of state aid to the kolkhoz construction,” which took full account of the varying conditions of the regions of the USSR. The Central Committee divided the country into three groups: the principal grain regions, best prepared, with the most tractors, sovkhozes and experience, were to complete collectivisation in the main by the spring of 1931; a second group of grain regions — the Ukraine, the Central Black Earth region, Siberia, the Urals and Kazakhstan — by the spring of 1932; the remaining regions, territories and republics could stretch the terms to the end of the Five-Year Plan, that is to 1933. The party simultaneously required the further acceleration of the building of the tractor and combine factories, while demanding a resolute rebuff to the tendencies that undervalued the role of horse traction in the current stage of the kolkhoz movement.

The kolkhoz movement and the correction of its distortions

On the basis of the liquidation of the kulaks and the establishment of wholesale collectivisation (сплошная коллективизация) a powerful kolkhoz movement unfolded. The peasants entered the kolkhozes by whole villages, districts and regions, sweeping the kulak from the land and freeing themselves from the kulak yoke. But alongside the great successes there began to appear shortcomings in the practice of the party workers — distortions (искривления) of the party’s policy in the kolkhoz construction. Despite the warning of the Central Committee, many party workers artificially forced the collectivisation, disregarding local conditions and the degree of preparedness of the peasants, and in a number of regions the principle of voluntariness was replaced by compulsion under threat of razkulachivanie and the loss of electoral rights. The Central Committee moved at once to correct the errors: by its decision the article of Stalin “Dizzy with Success” (Головокружение от успехов) was published on 2 March 1930, warning all those who, carried away by the successes, had fallen into crude errors and had sought to drive the peasants on the kolkhoz road by administrative pressure, and reasserting with full force the principle of voluntariness and the account of the diversity of the regions. The decision of 15 March 1930, “On the struggle with the distortions of the party line in the kolkhoz movement,” struck a further blow at the errors, and the article of 3 April 1930, “Reply to the kolkhoz comrades,” pointed to the root of the mistakes — the wrong approach to the middle peasant and the violation of the Leninist principle of voluntariness. By these measures the party achieved the liquidation of the distortions of its line in the kolkhoz movement.

The victory of the collective-farm system

The corrected movement advanced to its completion. In January 1933 the Central Committee resolved on the organisation of political departments at the machine-tractor stations (машинно-тракторные станции) serving the kolkhozes, and sent into the countryside seventeen thousand party workers for work in the political departments. In two years, 1933 and 1934, the political departments of the MTS did great work in removing the shortcomings in the life of the kolkhozes, in training the kolkhoz activists and strengthening the collective farms, and in clearing them of hostile, kulak and wrecking elements. The fulfilment of the Five-Year Plan in four years brought the victory of socialism on the whole front, and the Seventeenth Congress of the party, held in 1934, fixed the results: the collectivisation of agriculture, together with socialist industrialisation, had completed the construction of the foundation of socialist society. The school textbook of Shestakov taught the children of the Soviet land that socialist industry and the collective farms were the marks of the land of victorious socialism. The campaign of 1929–1934 was thus presented in the official accounts as the second great reconstruction of the country’s economy — the completion, in the village as in the town, of the building of socialism.
