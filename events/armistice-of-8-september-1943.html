The Armistice of 8 September 1943

8 September 1943

Italy’s surrender to the Allies, announced from the Rome broadcasting station on the evening of 8 September 1943, five weeks after the fall of Mussolini. By a secret armistice signed at Fairfield Camp in Sicily on 3 September, and published by General Eisenhower’s demand six days early, the government of Marshal badoglio passed Italy from the German to the Allied side of the war — a change of policy kept secret from the Germans until the moment of its announcement, and followed within hours by the German reaction that the new government had dreaded and could not meet. The armistice ended Italy’s war against the Allies and opened at once a war against her former partner; within days the King and the government would abandon Rome for Brindisi, leaving the capital to the Germans.

The secret negotiations

Within days of taking office the Badoglio government resolved that Italy must change sides, while professing to Berlin that the war would go on. The negotiations had to be kept so secret that they could not even be discussed in the Cabinet. On 29 July the new Minister of Foreign Affairs, Guariglia, the Ambassador returned from Turkey, arrived in Rome and entirely agreed that as few people as possible should know of them; he had already let it be known, through the Turkish Foreign Minister, that Italy was on the point of changing her policy. Communication with the Allies was the first difficulty: the British Minister to the Holy See had only a very old code almost certainly known to the Germans, and the American Chargé d’Affaires had none. It was agreed to reassure both sides at once — to accept a German invitation to a conference, and to send officials of the Foreign Office to Lisbon and Tangier to explain to the Allies that Italy accepted it only to lull the suspicions of the Germans. The conference met at Tarvisio on 6 August, in an atmosphere of doubt and suspicion, and reached no decisions; the Italian request, supported by General Ambrosio, to bring home the divisions in Russia and the Balkans was rejected. The only positive success was that the Allied press and wireless hardly mentioned the meeting.

The terms and the signing

For the actual negotiation, General Ambrosio proposed General Castellano, accompanied by a Foreign Office official who spoke perfect English, and it was agreed that a special train leaving for Lisbon on 12 August should carry the military mission. At Lisbon on 17 August the Allied staff chiefs handed him the terms of the armistice “to be accepted or rejected.” He returned to Rome on 28 August, and Badoglio spent a day examining terms that seemed to him very harsh and in some instances impossible to carry out, before laying them before the King. Castellano was instructed to explain that Italy, her armed forces so much weaker than the German, could not carry out the terms as they stood, and that the Government would conclude an armistice when the Allies had landed at least fifteen divisions in a suitable locality. General Bedell Smith replied that the terms communicated in Lisbon could not be altered — the Government could only accept or reject them; three or four divisions would first disembark on the mainland to draw off the German forces, followed a few days later by the fifteen divisions elsewhere; and if the terms were not accepted, all the cities, including Rome, would be razed to the ground. On 1 September Badoglio, Guariglia, Ambrosio and Castellano were all in favour of accepting; Badoglio went to the King for his orders, and Castellano returned to Sicily to sign. It was laid down that the date of the armistice would be between 10 and 15 September, probably 12 September; the documents were brought to Rome on 5 September, and the Allied High Command supplied a radio set with an American secret code, so that from 29 August the government was in constant touch with Allied Headquarters in Algiers.

The eve of 8 September

The plans for the day rested on the assumption that Rome could be held. Rome had been declared an “open city” (città aperta) — but the declaration was unilateral, never accepted by the British and Americans, though the military headquarters were moved outside a protected zone about seventeen miles round the city. Five divisions and the elements of two others, under General Carboni, were concentrated round the capital, and instructions had been drawn up for all commanding officers on their line of conduct in the event of an armistice. It had also been settled with Castellano and the Allied staffs that on the night of the armistice an airborne division should be landed on the outskirts of Rome and an armoured division at the mouth of the Tiber. But on the night of 7 September the plans began to break. About two in the morning of 8 September Carboni arrived at Badoglio’s house with the American General Taylor, who had come to arrange the landing of the airborne division and told Badoglio that, contrary to what had been said, the armistice was imminent and would perhaps be declared that very day. Badoglio objected that the airborne division could only begin its landing on the night of 9 September and could not coincide with the principal landing; Carboni said he would need days to complete the issue of ammunition and petrol. Badoglio drafted a telegram to General Eisenhower insisting that the armistice be postponed to 12 September, and during the morning General Rossi left at once by air for Allied Headquarters.

The announcement

At 5.30 p.m. on 8 September a telegram in code signed by General Eisenhower arrived requiring the Italian Government to declare the armistice at 8 p.m. — and declaring that if this was not done, the armistice signed on 3 September would no longer be valid and the negotiations would be broadcast from Algiers and London. Badoglio was astounded. He learned much later that the Allied High Command, well informed of the situation in Italy, had feared that his government would be overthrown by the Germans and had wished to expedite matters. “It was a decision which entirely upset our plans and brought us to the brink of ruin.” Eisenhower admitted later that evening, hearing from General Rossi the reasons for the request for postponement, “I am inclined to believe that I have made a mistake, but all that matters now is to collaborate to the best of our ability in our common interest.”

Badoglio went at once to the King with the ministers of the Army, the Navy and the Air Force, the Chiefs of the General Staff, Carboni and Major Marchesi. Ambrosio explained that the anticipation of the armistice found the troops in the act of taking up new positions; the Minister of the Army and Carboni, doubting that the British and Americans would keep their undertakings and fearing a violent German reaction, were in favour of rejecting it; Major Marchesi pointed out the serious harm of repudiating the conditions signed on 3 September; Guariglia declared that now more than ever it was essential to “go on to the end.” Badoglio set out the only two courses — the King might publicly repudiate what he had done and dismiss him, or they must accept the conditions whatever the consequences; and the King expressed the view that a change of policy was out of the question and that they must acquiesce. Meanwhile the information service warned that the B.B.C. had broadcast that Italy had asked for an armistice and that Eisenhower had declared that the Allied governments had agreed. It was not possible to delay a moment. The meeting broke up, and Badoglio went at once to the Rome broadcasting station and made the announcement: recognizing the impossibility of continuing the war in face of the overwhelming strength of the enemy, and in order to save the nation from further and even greater disasters, the Italian Government had asked General Eisenhower for an armistice; the request had been acceded to; all hostilities by the Italian armed forces against the British and American forces must cease, though they would repel attacks from whatever quarter they might come. The communiqué was recorded and repeated at frequent intervals, and the Minister of Foreign Affairs arranged to inform Berlin, Budapest, Bucharest and Sofia by telegram.

Aftermath

The armistice had been declared, but the German reaction that the government had so long feared was immediate. Within days the King, Badoglio and the government fled Rome for the Allied lines at Brindisi, leaving the capital — whose defence the plans of early September had been meant to secure — to the Germans, in the flight that forms the subject of Badoglio’s next chapter, “From Rome to Brindisi.” Italy’s passage from the German to the Allied side was complete in form, but the price was the German occupation of the peninsula and a new war, fought on Italian soil, that would last until the liberation of Rome in June 1944.
