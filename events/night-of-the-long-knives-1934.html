The Night of the Long Knives (30 June 1934)

30 June 1934

The action of 30 June through 2 July 1934 in which hitler, striking with the SS and the Gestapo, broke the leadership of the storm detachments and settled a wider account of blood — the deposition and murder of the SA chief of staff Ernst roehm, the shooting of General von Schleicher and of gregor-strasser, and the killing of others across the Reich that the regime would present as the suppression of a plot and retrospectively legalise as Staatsnotwehr (state self-defence). The purge removed the last internal force that stood between the Führer and absolute rule, and fixed the dictatorship in a month of murder.

The crisis of the storm detachments

Through the first months of 1934 the SA — the brown-shirted Sturmabteilung (storm detachment), two or three million strong — pressed against the limits the new regime had set it. Its chief of staff, Ernst Röhm, an old comrade of Hitler and the builder of the force, wanted the SA to be the nucleus of a new army of the revolution; the professional officers of the Reichswehr, who had helped put Hitler in power, demanded the SA’s reduction and the preservation of the army’s monopoly of arms. Heiden, writing a year later, describes the summer as an “unplanned dictatorship” sliding toward catastrophe: Röhm’s advances met with evasion, then with hostility; the Reichswehr leadership, with the field marshal President von Hindenburg behind it, made the demotion of the SA a condition of its continued support. Meanwhile voices around Röhm spoke openly of a “second revolution” that would complete what the first had left undone — a revolution that would sweep away the old officers, the conservatives and the men of the first hour who now stood in the way. The SA chief, whose “unfortunate disposition” the regime’s own documents would later cite, pressed his claims without discretion and entered, Heiden records, into dangerous contacts with General von Schleicher, the former chancellor, who had his own score to settle with the men who had displaced him.

The purge

On 30 June Hitler struck. He flew to Munich, then to the country retreat where Röhm and the SA leaders had gathered, and the arrests began; in Berlin, in the provinces, the SS and the secret police rounded up the brownshirt commanders through the night and shot many of them on the spot. Heiden’s chapter on the 30th of June is a catalogue of what an armed state does when it turns on its own party: the armoured cars and the pistol, the shootings of the SA men, the settling of accounts that had nothing to do with Röhm at all. General von Schleicher, shot in his house with his wife, was killed for the conspirator he had been or was alleged to be; Gregor Strasser, the fallen rival of the party’s left, was murdered in prison; conservative figures who had spoken against the regime — Heiden notes the Marburg speech and the resentment it drew — were swept up in the same bloodbath. Röhm himself was taken, and after Hitler refused to order his execution by his own hand, was shot in his cell on the following day. The purge ran on into the night of the first and the second of July, and the dead included, by the official tally later published, among others the SA chief of staff and a former Reich Chancellor.

The state’s account

The regime’s own documentary record of 1934 tells the event from the victor’s side, and it is instructive. The decree of 30 June deposed Röhm as chief of staff and expelled him from the SA and the party, and appointed the Obergruppenführer Lutze in his place; the Führer’s letter to Lutze spoke of the “gravest failings” of his predecessor. The published commentary enumerated those who had been shot — nineteen senior SA leaders, thirty-one SA leaders and men, three SS leaders, thirteen SA leaders and civilians who resisted arrest, and five party comrades, among them Röhm and General Schleicher — and set out the official charge: that the chief of staff, in collaboration with outside elements, had prepared a high-treasonous rising, and that the Führer and the government had been forced to the sharpest intervention. The law on measures of state self-defence of 3 July declared the measures taken on 30 June, 1 and 2 July to be “lawful as state self-defence” (als Staatsnotwehr rechtens); Hindenburg’s telegrams of 2 July thanked Hitler and conveyed the satisfaction of the President and the army; and in his speech to the Reichstag on 13 July Hitler, having described the plot, assumed full responsibility for the shootings and declared that during those twenty-four hours he had been the supreme court of the German people. The Reichswehr minister von Blomberg, in the cabinet, thanked Hitler for having saved the German people from civil war.

The meaning of the massacre

The two sources of the event could not diverge further, and the contrast is the history. For the regime the purge was a justified act of self-defence, the crushing of a treacherous plot by the SA leadership, crowned by Hindenburg’s approval and the applause of the army. For Heiden, writing from exile, it was a settling of accounts that revealed the nature of the power that had been built: an “end with terror,” the murder of the SA leaders who had served Hitler’s rise, of the prince who had once stood at his side, of Schleicher and Strasser and of nameless men shot for scores that had nothing to do with any plot. Heiden closes his biography not with the great victims but with a Munich lawyer, Dr. Glaser, shot by three SS men on the evening of 30 June — the ashes of the unknown German who fell victim, in his phrase, to the highest responsibility assumed on that day. The purge had done its work: the SA was tamed under Lutze, the army was bound to Hitler, and two months later, on the death of Hindenburg, the Führer united in his own person the offices of President and Chancellor, and the Wehrmacht swore its oath to him.
