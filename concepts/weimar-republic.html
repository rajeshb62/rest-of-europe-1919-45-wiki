The Weimar Republic

The German republic born of the revolution of November 1918, given its constitution at the National Assembly at Weimar in 1919, and carried through a decade of crisis and consolidation before its death in the autumn of 1930. Named for the Thuringian town in which its constitution was drafted, it is the political frame within which the National Socialist movement grew — the democratic state whose fall the movement both exploited and accomplished. The left-socialist historian Arthur Rosenberg, writing in exile in 1935, and the journalist Konrad Heiden, writing a year later, tell the story from opposite ends of the political divide, but they agree that the republic was the work of the working class and that it died because the classes that had made it were too divided to save it.

The birth of the republic

The republic was not proclaimed by a leader or a party but born of the collapse of the imperial order in the last weeks of the war. The revolution of 9 November 1918, Rosenberg writes, came from below — from the sailors, soldiers and workers who, in the face of the fleet’s last order to put to sea, refused the sacrifice and turned on their officers. In Berlin the workers’ and soldiers’ councils elected the new republican government in the Circus Busch on 10 November, the day after the monarchy fell. The first government, the Council of People’s Deputies (Rat der Volksbeauftragten), ruled with dictatorial power, composed of the three Social Democrat (SPD) and the three Independent (USPD) deputies of the revolutionary movement, governing through the winter of 1918–19 while the old order dissolved.

The revolution was not a single act but a contested one. In January 1919 the Spartacus League, which had founded itself as the Communist Party of Germany at its congress of 30 December 1918, rose against the republic it judged bourgeois, and the new government under its minister Gustav Noske suppressed the rising by force. The murders of Rosa Luxemburg and Karl Liebknecht in the suppression, Rosenberg writes, left a wound on the German left that never fully healed — a division between social democracy and communism that ran through the whole life of the republic. The republic, born of the working class, was from its first weeks fought over by two working classes.

The constitution at Weimar

The republic gave itself a legal form in the first months of 1919. Elections to a National Assembly were held on 19 January 1919, and the Assembly met not in stormy Berlin but in quiet Weimar, summoned — as Rosenberg puts it — to give the revolution its constitution and to show it new paths. The constitution drafted there made Germany a parliamentary democracy with a president elected by the people, a Reichstag elected by proportional representation, and the federal structure of the old Reich reshaped into a unitary state, guaranteeing the classic rights of the citizen and the social claims of the age — the right to work, to education, to welfare — as the product of a revolution made in the name of the people.

The constitution was the achievement of the democratic centre, above all of the Social Democrats and the democratic parties that had come to the front in November 1918. Rosenberg’s central point is that this legal structure rested on a social foundation that was already cracked: the bourgeois parties, which had governed imperial Germany, either fought the republic or supported it lukewarmly; the army, the bureaucracy and the great landowners remained monarchist at heart; and the working class that had made the revolution was divided between the social democrats who defended it and the communists who denounced it. The constitution was real, but the will to defend it was concentrated in a minority.

The crises of the early years

The republic spent its first five years in a state of siege. In March 1920 the counter-revolution struck in the Kapp Putsch — the attempt of nationalist officers and civil servants, led by the East Prussian official Wolfgang Kapp, to seize the state and undo the republic. The putsch failed not because the republican government defended itself but because a general strike of the workers, called by the trade unions and the Social Democrats, paralysed the country and forced the conspirators out. Rosenberg draws the lesson: the republic was saved by the working class acting outside the state, while the state’s own forces — the army, the bureaucracy — had stood aside or gone over.

The early 1920s were dominated by the burden of the Entente’s demands. The period 1920–1922 Rosenberg calls the era of the “Catholic democracy” (katholische Demokratie), when the Centre party and its democratic-Catholic partners held the balance and the reparations question pressed on every government. The pressure culminated in 1923, the republic’s worst year. The French occupied the Ruhr; the Reich, under the shipping magnate Cuno who had replaced the fallen Wirth, answered with a policy of passive resistance; and the currency collapsed in the catastrophic inflation that devoured the savings of the middle classes. Rosenberg’s account of 1923 is of a republic dissolving under the double burden of foreign occupation and internal ruin — the year in which the nationalist Right, and with it the young Hitler’s movement, drew its first serious strength.

The stabilisation under Stresemann

From the wreckage of 1923 the republic was rebuilt under Gustav Stresemann. The currency was stabilised, the passive resistance ended, and the Dawes Plan brought American money and a new settlement of the reparations question. The years 1924–1928 were the republic’s period of consolidation, when the crisis receded and the republic seemed to have survived. Rosenberg, writing from the left, gives the era a sharper edge: it was the period in which the American financial power that had emerged from the war as the first power of the earth underwrote the republic, and in which the generals and the capitalists, who had never accepted the democratic state, waited and worked for its reversal.

Heiden’s biography fills in the other side of the same years. The republic of the mid-1920s was a state in which the National Socialist movement, shattered by the Beer Hall Putsch of 1923, was refounded and rebuilt through the legal channels the constitution made available. Hitler’s decision after the putsch — that power was not to be stormed but awaited, through the state and the ballot — was a judgment on the republic as a machine that could be captured by its own forms. The years of relative quiet were the years in which the movement gathered the strength the crisis of 1929 would release.

The death of the republic

The end came swiftly after the crisis of 1929 turned the republic’s stabilisation into ruin. The collapse of the American financial system cut off the money on which the republic had rested, and the depression brought unemployment and despair to the masses the constitution had promised to protect. The National Socialists, twelve seats in the Reichstag of 1928, became the mass party of protest — a hundred and seven seats in the elections of 14 September 1930. Rosenberg fixes that election as the death of the republic. In the autumn of 1930 the Reich government under Brüning was set up as a presidential cabinet governing by emergency decree, no longer resting on a parliamentary majority; and on 18 October 1930 the Reichstag, summoned to vote, capitulated and passed the emergency measures. Rosenberg calls this “the death-hour of the Weimar Republic” (die Todesstunde der Weimarer Republik).

The republic died, in Rosenberg’s reading, because its fate was entrusted to the bourgeoisie while a divided working class could not save it. The men who governed it from 1930 — Brüning, and after him Papen, Schleicher and finally the National Socialists — were the men of the classes that had fought the republic from its birth. The constitution was suspended from within, by legal means, before it was abolished; the Reichstag voted its own power away in the Enabling Act of 1933, and the republic born in revolution perished without a revolution to defend it. For Rosenberg and Heiden alike, it is the cautionary term of the interwar age — a democracy made by a divided people, whose division was the instrument of its death.
