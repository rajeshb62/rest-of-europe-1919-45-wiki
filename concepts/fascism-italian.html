Italian Fascism

Fascism (Fascismo) is the movement, the doctrine and the regime that rose in Italy from the interventionism of the First World War and seized power with the March on Rome of October 1922. The name comes from the fascio (bundle), the Roman symbol of strength in union, which the first militants adopted for their bands — the Fasci di combattimento (combat groups) founded at the Piazza San Sepolcro in Milan on 23 March 1919. The four Italian sources in this wiki — the movement-insider origin-story of Alberto Businelli, the academic regime history of Gioacchino Volpe, the official party chronicle of Giorgio Pini and Federico Bresadola, and the exhaustive documentary history of the Cremona boss Roberto Farinacci — together present Fascism as it understood itself from within: a revolution, not a change of government, born of the war, directed against the liberal and socialist order, and carried to power by the energy of its young blackshirts under the will of Benito Mussolini.

The war and the Fasci

All four narratives root the movement in the First World War and the interventionist crisis. Volpe’s History of the Fascist Movement opens the story with the interventionist split of 1914–15, when Mussolini, the editor of Il Popolo d’Italia, broke with the Socialist party and its neutrality to carry the campaign for Italy’s entry into the war. Businelli’s Rivoluzione fascista, prefaced by the futurist Filippo Tommaso Marinetti, recounts the same turn from the Balkan powder-keg of 1912–1914 through the declaration of Italian neutrality in August 1914 and the founding of Il Popolo d’Italia, and celebrates the young Futurist-squadrist energies that answered the call. Farinacci, writing from the radical wing, casts the whole crisis year of 1919 as the collapse of the ruling class — the corruption of the liberal governments and the diplomatic humiliation at Paris — against which the Fasci were the patriotic answer. The war is not, in these accounts, a catastrophe to be repented but the furnace in which the new Italy was forged; the demobilised officers and officer-students whom Volpe names among the movement’s founders carried its temper into the postwar.

The Fasci di combattimento, founded at Milan on 23 March 1919, are treated by every source as the decisive act. Mussolini himself, in these accounts, presented the new formation not as a party but as an “anti-party” — an instrument of the youth, the impulse and the faith that the old political world had lost. Volpe stresses this character of the movement as against the party: it was defined by energy, pragmatism and devotion to the nation rather than by a fixed programme, and its programme, drawn up at the congress of Florence, remained subordinate to the action itself. Farinacci devotes his first volume to the year of the founding, tracing the creation of the Fasci, the assault on the offices of the socialist daily Avanti!, and the place of the Arditi and the interventionists in the new formation. The national humiliation over Fiume and the Adriatic, denied to Italy at the peace conference, supplies the patriotic grievance that the Fasci take up.

The movement, squadrism and the March on Rome

The passage from the parliamentary-political phase of 1919 to the violent phase of 1920–1921 is the turning of the story. Confronted with the red tide of the biennio rosso — the strikes, the agrarian unrest and the occupation of the factories of September 1920 — the Fasci answered with the squadre d’azione (action squads), the armed bands of blackshirts whose punitive expeditions (the spedizioni punitive) broke the Socialist and the labour organisations in the Po valley and the cities. Pini’s chronicle devotes a chapter to the camicie nere and to squadrism, telling how Fascism organised itself in the provinces against the collapse of the liberal order; Farinacci, in the second volume of his Storia della Rivoluzione Fascista, presents the “Victory of the Fasci” as the patriotic, anti-Bolshevik answer to the “Red Insurrection” of 1920, and defends the squads as a surgical necessity forced on the nation by the impotence of the state. The movement’s own historians do not soften this violence; they vindicate it, casting it as the reassertion of Italianness against the foreign and the red, and the “drip of blood” (the stillicidio di sangue) of the martyrs as the price of the resurrection.

The March on Rome of October 1922 is the climax every source narrates. Businelli’s sixth chapter, “The Year of Victory,” describes the plan of insurrection, the gathering of the Fasci at Naples on 24 October, and the concentration of the columns upon the capital; Farinacci’s third volume carries the account through the fall of the Giolittian, Bonomi and Facta ministries to the King’s refusal to order a military response and the entry of the blackshirts into Rome on 28 October. Volpe, soberly, records the quadrumvirate of action — Bianchi, De Vecchi, Balbo and General De Bono — given a dictatorial mandate, the massing of a hundred thousand blackshirts, and the King’s summons of Mussolini, who rose to power “by revolutionary means.” In every telling the March is the victory not of an election but of the movement’s own will, the nation’s ideal revolt against a state that could no longer govern.

Fascism at the helm

The sources present the years after 1922 as the consolidation of the regime and the building of a new state. Volpe’s chapter “Fascism at the Helm” records the restoration of order, the return of the state budget to balance, the public works and the agricultural campaign (the battaglia del grano), and the advance of Italy’s place among the nations; Pini’s later chapters carry the story through the “proof” of the Matteotti murder and the Aventine secession of 1924, and then the “Conquest of the State” of 1925–1927 — the dictatorship of 3 January, the works of the regime, the Fascist economy, the syndical organisation and the Labour Charter. The theme, in every account, is that Fascism did not merely hold office but transformed the state: it replaced the old parliamentary liberalism with a unified national order, expressed in the Fascist militia, the party reform, and ultimately the corporate organisation of the economy. Volpe, in his Author’s Note of 1936, surveys what two further years had added — the new cities, the land reclamation, the colonial development, and above all the corporate structure — as the natural fruit of the same revolution.

A doctrine of action

What the sources give is less a doctrine than a temper. Fascism appears as the party of the nation against the parties, the faith of the young against the old, action against talk, sacrifice against interest. Businelli calls the movement’s energy a “bollente fede italiana” (a burning Italian faith); Pini ascribes the state’s formation to popular adhesion, the dedication of martyrs and “the genial and victorious will of Benito Mussolini”; Volpe presents Fascism as a “revolution” that offers itself to Europe, a civilising mission the other nations must learn from. Farinacci, the most polemical, frames it as the revenge of the honest and the patriotic upon a corrupted ruling class. Across all of them runs the conviction that Fascism was not one more party contending for office but the awakening of the Italian nation itself — the latest-formed of the European states, in Pini’s closing words, certain of its imperial future.
