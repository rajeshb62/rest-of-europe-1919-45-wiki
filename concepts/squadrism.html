Squadrism

Squadrism (squadrismo) is the armed phase of the Fascist movement — the organisation and action of the squadre d’azione (action squads), the bands of blackshirts who, in the years 1920–1921, carried the Fascist counter-offensive against the socialist and labour movements of Italy. The word names at once the squads themselves and the style of politics they embodied: the punitive expedition (spedizione punitiva), the beating of opponents, the burning of the offices of socialist newspapers, the ejection of elected councils, and the claim of blood and martyrdom as the proof of the nation’s faith. The sources of this wiki — Businelli’s origin-story, Pini’s party chronicle, Volpe’s regime history, and above all Farinacci’s party history of the red biennium — present squadrism from within, as the movement itself understood it: the patriotic and anti-Bolshevik reaction of a people defending its Italianness against the red tide.

The squads and the red tide

The squadre d’azione grew out of the confrontation between the Fasci and the workers’ and peasants’ movement of 1919–1920. The postwar years had brought the biennio rosso (the red biennium): waves of strikes, agrarian unrest in the Po valley, the seizure of factories by the metalworkers in September 1920, and the dominion of the Socialist and labour leagues over wide provinces. Businelli’s Rivoluzione fascista narrates the turn as the “tragic year of recovery” — the strikes and the red wave, the occupation of the factories, and the Fascist reaction that followed. Farinacci, in the second volume of his Storia della Rivoluzione Fascista, subtitled L’Insurrezione Rossa e la Vittoria dei Fasci, presents the same months in two parts: first the “Red Insurrection” of the workers and peasants, and then the “Victory of the Fasci” that answered it. For him the squads were not aggressors but the necessary response to anarchy — the nation’s armed defence against a revolution that the liberal state, from Nitti to Giolitti, was too weak or too cowardly to stop. The movement’s own historians never disavow the violence; they justify it as the reassertion of order and of the national will.

The punitive expeditions

The characteristic action of squadrism was the punitive expedition — the spedizione punitiva in which a column of blackshirts descended upon a town or a quarter to break the strength of the red organisations. Farinacci’s chapter on the action squads and the reprisals (Le squadre d’azione e le rappresaglie) records the multiplication of the Fasci, the end of the strength of the state, and the punitive expeditions launched against the revolutionary organisations, which he describes as a “surgical necessity” — a phrase that captures the movement’s own reading of its violence as a healing act. The same chapter tells of the rising of Trieste against the arrogance of Slavs and Socialists, the burning of the Balkan socialist Lavoratore, the barricades and massacres of the Venezia Giulia, and the devastation of the red strongholds. Pini’s chronicle, in its chapter on the camicie nere, recounts the same campaign in the provinces — the battles of Sarzana and Modena, the exhaustion of the liberal government — as squadrism organising itself into a force that could no longer be resisted. The stillicidio di sangue, the drip of blood that the movement’s martyrology counted in its fallen, is treated in every account as the sacrificial currency of the resurrection: blood called to blood, and each martyr made the faith more certain.

The conquest of the localities

Squadrism was, above all, a local conquest. The squads did not merely fight the reds in the streets; they seized the communes. Volpe’s History of the Fascist Movement records the “battles of local expurgation” by which the blackshirts ejected the Socialist mayors and communal councils and installed Fascist administration, and notes how the party and the squadre became one — the Party and the squads as a single organism. Farinacci’s account of the “epicentre” of the Fascist resurgence — the province of Bologna, the capital of what he calls agricultural Bolshevism — tells the same story in detail: the massacre of Molinella, the dominion of the red leagues over the proprietors, and the tragic episode of the Palazzo d’Accursio in Bologna, where the installation of the new communal council ended in the killing of a Fascist and the sounding of the tocsin. The expulsion of the legitimate socialist councils was in many provinces the decisive Fascist act before the March on Rome; by the time of the national elections of May 1921, into which the Fascists entered as part of the National Blocs, the movement had already reconquered the countryside commune by commune.

The civil war and its meaning

The sources treat the years 1920–1921 as a civil war — and they say so openly. Farinacci speaks of a “phase of the civil war” in the electoral struggle of 1921, of assassinations and reactions, of the law of the talion and of a “bloody dripping” (un sanguinoso stillicidio), and of the rovent (glowing) atmosphere of the battle. Businelli calls 1920 the “bloody Christmas” and the year of martyrdom; Pini entitles his treatment of 1920 the convulsione insanguinata (the blood-stained convulsion). Yet in every one of these accounts the violence is narrated as the nation’s self-defence and self-renewal: the squads are the camicie nere (the blackshirts) of the new Risorgimento, the armed conscience of Italy against a state that had abandoned it. The meaning the movement attached to squadrism was not terror but resurrection — the reassertion of Italianness, of the land, and of national honour against the foreign and the red. That is why the same sources who record the burnings and the beatings also record the martyrs’ names, and why the squadrist, with his black shirt and his dagger, stands at the centre of the Fascist origin-story as the type of the new Italian.

From squads to the regime

With the March on Rome of October 1922 the squads had conquered the state, and their place in the movement changed. Volpe records how the party, the militia and the quasi-constitutional union of the new government drew the armed energies of squadrism within the state, and how the restoration of order became the task of the regime. The squadrist temper did not vanish — Farinacci, the most unrepentant of the ras, remained a force in the party through the Matteotti crisis and the consolidation of 1925 — but the squads were progressively absorbed into the institutions of the Fascist state, the party and the Militia. The sources present this as the consummation of squadrism rather than its suppression: the violence that had made the nation again was now the authority that ruled it. The squadre d’azione had done their work, and the movement that had armed them took up the governance of the corporate state.
