National Socialism

The ideology and political movement that seized power in Germany in 1933 — at once a racial doctrine, a theory of the state, and a mass organisation built on the leadership principle and the spoken word. National Socialism (Nationalsozialismus) is named in the title of its party, the National Socialist German Workers’ Party (NSDAP), a name Hitler chose to unite the nationalist and the socialist currents of postwar Germany. Its doctrinal foundation is laid out in Hitler’s own Mein Kampf; its critical anatomy in Konrad Heiden’s contemporary biography; and its racial-mystical voice, in the weeks after the seizure of power, in Hans Geisow’s völkisch tract. The three sources together show an ideology that presents itself as the opposite of the liberal and Marxist world that preceded it, built on race, blood and the leadership principle.

The doctrine of race and the völkisch state

At the core of National Socialist doctrine, as Hitler sets it out in Mein Kampf, stands the race. The state is not, as in liberal or Marxist thought, a contract among citizens or an instrument of class, but the means by which a people preserves its racial being. The chapter “Volk und Rasse” (People and Race) lays down the hierarchy of races, the creative power of the superior race, and the duty of the state to keep the blood pure. The Jew appears as the contrary principle — the force that dissolves race, undermines the state, and stands behind both Marxism and the liberalism that National Socialism attacks. Hitler’s political awakening, he insists, was his discovery in Vienna of the “Jewish question,” and the whole doctrine turns on it: the history of Germany’s collapse in 1918 is told as the work of the “November criminals” and the Jewish-Marxist influence that stabbed the army in the back.

The state that follows from this doctrine is the völkische Staat (völkisch state). The second volume of Mein Kampf gives it its form: race as the foundation, the rejection of the democratic and parliamentary state in which numbers and majorities rule, and a division of the people into subjects and citizens on racial grounds. The citizen is not the man who happens to live within the borders of the state but the man of the right race who is willing and able to serve it. The state’s purpose is the preservation and advancement of the racial people, and every institution — law, economy, education, culture — is to be bent to that end. This is the doctrine that the official documentary compilations of the regime would later present as realised in law: the citizenship law of July 1933, the rebuilding of state and economy, the racial policy that Geisow’s tract exalts as the “soul” of the new Reich.

The movement and its meaning

National Socialism is not only a doctrine but a movement — and the movement is prior to the state. Mein Kampf distinguishes Weltanschauung (worldview) from party and insists that the worldview, once accepted, must conquer the state through an organisation bound to it. The party Hitler built was the instrument of the doctrine: a mass organisation of the spoken word, held together by the Führerprinzip (leadership principle) by which authority flows downward from an absolute chief and every man answers to his superior while being relieved of responsibility toward those below. Heiden, who attended National Socialist meetings from 1922, offers the sharpest contemporary reading of what this meant. The movement, he writes, offered the German middle classes “relief from responsibility through an absolute supreme authority — security and moral comfort”; it gathered the failed, the resentful, and the credulous into a community that promised to reverse their ruin and restore the nation’s pride.

Heiden’s picture of the movement is the critical counterpart to Hitler’s own. Where Mein Kampf presents a doctrine of racial renewal, Heiden shows a machine built of oratory, symbols and the cultivation of mass feeling — wit, logic and insolence, the hammering repetition of a few propositions, the listener made into a collaborator. “Hitler always says the same thing,” Heiden quotes, and the sameness was the instrument. The movement’s growth from the tiny German Workers’ Party, which Hitler entered as its seventh member in the Munich of 1919, to the mass party of the early 1930s was the work of this propaganda and organisation — the red banner, the Hakenkreuz (swastika), the storm troopers of the SA, the twenty-five-point programme. Heiden’s biography traces the “steps to power”: the Beer Hall Putsch of 1923, the seven years of reconstruction and electoral advance after 1925, and the capture of the state in 1933.

The völkisch soul of the Third Reich

The racial doctrine that Mein Kampf argues is given a theological-racial form in Geisow’s tract of February 1933, written in the weeks after Hitler’s appointment as Chancellor. Die Seele des Dritten Reiches (The Soul of the Third Reich) presents the National Socialist revolution as “a piece of religion” — the awakening of the German people’s inherited being against the foreign, dismembering forces that had corrupted it. Geisow’s opening chapter, “Weltenwende” (Turn of the Age), holds that history is a piece of nature and that the forces of race and blood, not political arrangement, are the true currents beneath events. The Volksgemeinschaft (national community) that overcomes class divisions is the goal; the enemy is liberalism — that alien, artfremd way of viewing life born of the French Revolution and carried by Jewish-masonic forces — and with it the individualist, dismembering order that had dissolved the people into its parts.

Geisow applies the doctrine across the whole of life: thought itself is race-bound (arteigen), and the German must think out of his own inherited being rather than in alien categories; the economy must be restored to its bond with the people, not left to liberal world-economy; the family is the innermost cell of the people’s life; the church must grasp the blood-bound, God-given order of the people. The tract closes as it opens, with God making world history through the nature and blood He gives to men and peoples — to be German is to be a whole man, and the German’s struggle for his right to life is a piece of religion. Where Mein Kampf gives National Socialism its political and racial argument, Geisow gives it its soul — the conviction of the movement’s own adherents in its first months that the revolution was not a change of government but the reawakening of a race.

National Socialism as doctrine and power

The sources agree on the shape of the whole, whatever their standpoint. For Hitler, National Socialism is the true doctrine of the German people, the worldview that must conquer the state and prepare the nation’s racial future — including the demand for Lebensraum (living space) in the East that the closing foreign-policy chapters of Mein Kampf set out. For Heiden, writing from the critical-democratic side, it is “the age of irresponsibility” made flesh — the reactionary revolution of a middle class that had abandoned responsibility and found, in the leadership principle, relief from the burden of freedom. For Geisow, from within the movement, it is the soul of the German people awakened against the alien. The three voices together — the founder’s doctrine, the opponent’s anatomy, the adherent’s faith — give the fullest contemporary portrait of the ideology that shaped the German century.
