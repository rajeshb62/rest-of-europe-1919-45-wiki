The French Third Republic

The parliamentary republic that governed France from the fall of the Second Empire in September 1870 until the military collapse of 1940 — the longest-lived regime France had known since the Revolution, and the frame within which the political, social and moral life of the interwar years was lived. Born of the defeat at Sedan and consolidated only after a decade of monarchist crisis, it is the very model of the Latin parliamentary republic of the centre that this collection of sources sets against the revolutionary and totalitarian states of Russia, Germany, Italy and Spain. Three of the five French sources that ground this wiki tell its story from within — Alexandre Zévaès, a Socialist deputy who had sat in the Chamber from 1898 to 1914; André Siegfried, the electoral geographer writing in 1930; and Marc Bloch, the historian of the Annales school who drew the regime’s balance in the ruin of 1940 — and between them they give the republic as its defenders and its critics saw it, from its birth to its death.

The birth of the regime

The republic was proclaimed on the 4th of September 1870, in the week of the catastrophe of the Second Empire. Zévaès, writing as a man of the Left, opens his history with the empire of the césarisme of Soulouque — the police-ridden, censored state of fashionable Paris, the muzzled press and the ubiquitous mouchard (spy) — whose gaudy luxury masked the rot that Sedan exposed. The proclamation of the republic in the Hôtel de Ville followed the surrender of the Emperor, and the new government, the Government of National Defence, at once confronted the war against Prussia and the negotiation of the peace. Within the year the regime passed through its most terrible trial: the insurrection of the Commune in the spring of 1871, its bloody repression, and the election of a National Assembly in which a monarchist majority threatened to undo the republic in its cradle. The republic of the first decade hung on the hazard of events — the republican campaign of Gambetta, the fall of Thiers on the Vingt-quatre mai, the Ordre moral of Marshal MacMahon and the duc de Broglie with its religious pilgrimages and its veiled royalism — before the Constitution of 1875 fixed the republican form in law.

The constitution gave France a president and two chambers, a Senate and a Chamber of Deputies elected by universal suffrage; and it is this parliamentary frame that Siegfried, the geographer, takes as the permanent object of his analysis. Writing in 1930, he treats the regime not as a recent construction but as the political expression of a national character already complete by the end of the eighteenth century — peasant and artisan, individualist, “hard as what is old,” formed before the industrial age that was now closing around it. The parties of the republic, in his reading, issue from two revolutions: the French Revolution, which set the fundamental division of the political spectrum, and the Industrial Revolution, which laid a newer social-economic fault across it. And the temperament underneath, he insists, is intimate contradiction — the Frenchman is politically on the left but socially conservative; his heart is on the left and his pocket on the right.

The consolidation of the republic

The republic that had so narrowly survived its first years became, after 1879, the settled form of French government. Zévaès carries the narrative through the great crises that tested and ultimately confirmed the regime: the Boulangist danger, when the Republic seemed momentarily to hang on the popularity of a single general; the Panama scandal, which exposed the corruption of parliamentary finance; and above all the Dreyfus affair, which for years split France into two camps and, in its resolution, produced the consolidation of the republic under Waldeck-Rousseau and the ministry of Combes — the law on associations, the dispersal of the religious congregations, and in 1905 the separation of Church and State. The republic of the radicals, in Zévaès’s sympathetic account, was the regime of the working class and the small producers of the civisme (civic-mindedness) that Siegfried names as a permanent trait of the French character. Its great legislative age, from the 1880s to the eve of the war, was the age in which the republic made itself a social state.

The war of 1914–1918, which the republic carried through the union sacrée (sacred union) of all parties, left it victorious but exhausted. The treaties were signed, the peace won; and yet Zévaès, closing his history in 1926, draws a sombre balance. The immense problems of the peace — the application of the treaty, the restoration of the devastated regions, the crushing debts and the ruinous rise in the cost of living — confronted a political class of evident infirmity. He evokes the spectacle of a life stripped of grandeur, the rivalry of ambitions and vanities in the multiplied groups of the Chamber, and, behind the paravent of the great war in which fifteen hundred thousand men had fallen for Right and Civilization, the furious unloosing of appetites and cupidities. The politics of the Directory and the morals of the Directory: such is the portrait he leaves of the postwar republic at the moment his own narrative ends.

The character of the regime

Siegfried, writing four years later, gives the regime its analytical anatomy. Its permanent spirit, he argues, is made of three persistences and three reactions. The persistences are the affirmation of popular sovereignty, declared irrevocable; an incorrigible penchant for absolutism, the tenacious virus of the past that reappears nine times out of ten in those who administer or govern — for, as Alain’s maxim runs, power is always monarchical; and the Bonapartist spirit, a nostalgia for power in a regime that systematically enervates it, whence a latent antiparliamentarism in a country that precisely willed parliamentarism to be everything. Because the people wish to be everything, their elected delegate becomes the essential piece of the system: the supremacy of the deputy, of universal suffrage. And because government is enervated by its very nature, the republic lives in a constant oscillation of ministries, majorities and scrutins (division votes).

This is the regime whose end Halévy’s chronicle and Bloch’s indictment record. Daniel Halévy, writing the year-by-year diary of 1938, finds in the practice of the parliamentary republic a governing art of drift and inaction — the pratique du rien, the practice of nothing, as he names it. Bloch, writing in the fury of 1940, draws the same regime to its account. His examen de conscience (examination of conscience) of a Frenchman indicts not the republic alone but the whole society that had failed to prepare for total war — the military caste, the political class, the bourgeoisie and its “old demon,” the educational system. Yet even at the moment of death Bloch does not abandon the regime’s deepest claim. In the clandestine essay he wrote under Vichy, Pourquoi je suis républicain, he makes his confession: the republic is the form of power that leaves the citizen the object of a mature choice, the community that does not impose itself upon man. The regime that governed France for seventy years died not because it had always been false but because, at the crisis, the society that should have defended it had ceased to believe in it. That is the strange defeat, and the balance-sheet of the Third Republic, as its own historians and witnesses drew it.
