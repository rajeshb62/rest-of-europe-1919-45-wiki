Miguel Primo de Rivera

The Pronunciamiento

Miguel Primo de Rivera, a general of the Spanish Army, entered Madrid in the autumn of 1923 carrying the resolution to make himself dictator. The pronunciamiento (military coup) of 13 September 1923, which he had long been preparing from his command, found a country exhausted by the social war in Barcelona, by the trauma of the disaster of Annual in Morocco, and by a parliamentary system that seemed to produce only short-lived ministries. Fernández Almagro’s account of the last constitutional government records the general’s impatience, the denounced conspiracies, the sedition in Málaga, and the Central Staff memorandum, until “Primo de Rivera on the way” resolved the crisis by marching. The King accepted him; the constitution was suspended, and the Dictatorship began under a Directorio militar (Military Directory) in which the general ruled with the collaboration of generals and, later, of civilian ministers.

The Military and Civil Dictatorships

The Dictatorship ran in two phases. The first, from 1923 to 1925, was the Directorio militar, a purely military government. Fernández Almagro follows its measures: the first decrees, the appointment of Martínez Anido to Gobernación, the courtship of the socialists against the politicians, the confinement of Unamuno, the Unión Patriótica (Patriotic Union) as the régime’s single movement, the exile of the Marqués de Cortina, and above all the Moroccan question. The dictator’s great triumph was the liquidation of the Rif war: the Hispano-French cooperation, the landing at Alhucemas in 1925, and the final surrender of Abd-El-Krim, which erased the nightmare of Annual and gave the Dictatorship its one unqualified success. Madariaga’s judgment, however, is that even this success did not redeem the régime: in his balance-sheet the Dictatorship left Spain in a situation equal to or worse than that which it found — “En lo esencial, la dictadura dejó a España en una situación igual o peor que la que encontró.” The second phase, from 1925 to 1930, was the government of civilian collaborators under the dictator’s presidency, culminating in the Asamblea Nacional Consultiva (National Consultative Assembly) of 1927 and the draft of a new constitution that the Crown and the country were unwilling to receive.

The Encounter with Mussolini

The Dictatorship was the Spanish instance of the interwar turn toward authoritarian government, and Primo de Rivera himself sought the parallel. Fernández Almagro records the dictator’s journey to Rome and the encounter with Mussolini, the meeting that linked the Spanish directorio to Italian Fascism in the imagination of contemporaries. The personal affinity was real but the regimes differed; the Italian visit gave the Dictatorship an aura of European currency, of belonging to the movement of the age, even as its substance remained a Spanish military paternalism. The Unión Patriótica was a state party rather than a fascist militia, and the Dictatorship made its peace with the Church and the established interests more readily than any revolutionary movement would have done. The Roman episode nevertheless fixed Primo de Rivera in the wiki’s European pattern of the crisis of liberal order, a Spanish variant beside the Italian and later German cases.

The Erosion and Fall

From 1928 the Dictatorship visibly decayed. Fernández Almagro narrates the accumulating signs: the social question and the schools, the socialists already thinking of the Dictatorship’s end, the exile of Sánchez-Guerra, the artillery pronunciamiento that failed, the dissolution of the Artillery corps, the death of Queen María Cristina, the student strike against Article 53 of the University Statute, the depreciation of the peseta. Madariaga’s chapter on the liquidation insists that no exact judgment of the Dictatorship is possible without a balanced opinion of the régime that it had pleased to call “ancient,” and that the collapse of the one brought down the other. When the Dictatorship fell in January 1930, Primo de Rivera resigned and the King returned to constitutional government under General Berenguer, but the monarchical machinery was too damaged to recover. The dictator, who had bound the Crown to his experiment, went into exile in Paris, where he died a few weeks later, in March 1930 — Fernández Almagro records his “last official note” and the account of the end of the Dictatorship that followed.
