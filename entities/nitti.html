Francesco Saverio Nitti

The Man of the Ruling Class

The chronicle of the Fascist rise treats the premiership of Francesco Saverio Nitti as the emblem of everything it set out to overturn — the Governo senza fede (Government without Faith), the title the authors give the year 1919–20. When the Orlando cabinet fell in June 1919, Nitti, an economist and man of the southern provinces, succeeded it. The regime’s historians concede the welcome he received: he enjoyed a reputation for intellect and culture, and it was believed he also possessed the energy that Italy so needed after the strain of the war. Pini’s chronicle records that in such a moment a Government able to speak a word of faith could have steadied the nation, and that many hoped the new premier was capable of it — only to watch, within a few weeks, every illusion collapse. “To Francesco Nitti,” the chronicle concludes, “there lacked absolutely the faith in the destinies of Italy”; an arid mentality of the bad economist, he stood discouraged before the enormous liabilities of the war. Farinacci, writing the authoritative party history a decade later, finds the same verdict in the record of the summer of 1919: after Caporetto Nitti had invoked a “greater sense of reality” (maggior senso di realtà), which the Fascist historian dismisses as merely the more cautious variant of the Giolittian parecchio — the readiness to cede whatever the enemy and the Allies demanded, so long as peace was preserved.

The Illusions of the First Weeks

The chronicles dwell on how quickly the first reputation faded. In the assessment of the regime’s historians, Nitti’s cunning was after the method of the old Giolitti, yet where Giolitti, in his way of handling men, never abandoned the dignity of the statesman, in Nitti the same astuteness was ready to decline into gross attempts at corruption and shameless mendacity. The test came almost at once. At the end of July 1919 the Socialist party ordered a great general strike, which Nitti faced with an enormous deployment of force, for a moment winning himself the fame of an energetic man and a full applause in the Senate; it was afterwards understood that the measures had been solicited privately by many of the Socialist parliamentary group, with whom Nitti was on excellent personal terms. The Italian Socialists, the chronicle observes, began to fear they would have to run the real risk of making the revolution in earnest. To the Fascist historians this intimacy between the premier and his declared enemies was not a sign of balance but of the very collapse of the state. Farinacci’s account of “Nitti al Governo” is built on the same indictment: the “blackmail-note” of Wilson, the programme of peace at any price, the prayers of a Prefect, and the massacre of via Nazionale in Rome, where the state’s own weakness issued in blood in the capital.

The Months of Shame

It was the Fiume undertaking that fixed Nitti’s place in the Fascist narrative. Pini titles the chapter of his ministry “The Gesture of D’Annunzio — Months of Shame” and treats the Adriatic crisis as the moment when the premier’s prudence was exposed as something worse. Farinacci’s Chapter “Fiume contro Roma” — Fiume against Rome — names its theme directly as “the cowardice of Nitti,” set against the epic of Ronchi and the march of the Legionaries. In this telling the Government of official Italy could respond to the burning city of the oltraggiata Fiume only with inertia, while the man at its head, cautious “up to cowardice” in his action of command, was outmatched by the greatness of events; his indifference, once derided, now sounded as an outrage to the men of the war. Businelli’s movement narrative records the same indignation from within the Fascio: on 9 June the Fascio of Milan admonished the Italian delegates at the conference of Paris to follow a line intransigently Italian and placed its veto on the return of Nitti and of Giolitti; when Orlando abandoned power and Nitti succeeded him, Fascism was not yet strong enough to impose its decisions, but it was no longer weak enough to accept the accomplished fact.

The Fall and the Legacy

The second volume of Farinacci’s history, treating the “red insurrection” and the victory of the Fasci, devotes its eighteenth and nineteenth chapters to “Nitti al Governo” and “La caduta di Nitti” — the fall of Nitti. The Fascist historian presents the ministry’s agony as the slow transformation of minds and the sad agony of the ruling class, the premiere of the bourgeois chief whose policy of peace at any price surrendered the national honour while the country drifted into the anarchy of the biennio rosso. When the premiership passed in June 1920 to the returned Giolitti, the chroniclers saw the substitution as the exchange of one failing system for another. For the men who wrote the regime’s history, Nitti stood for the entire generation of statesmen who had won the war and then lost the peace — the classe dirigente whose sfacelo (collapse) the first part of Farinacci’s work is organized to demonstrate, and against which the Fasci di combattimento had been founded as the patriotic answer. It is precisely this portrait — the intelligent, faithless economist governing through caution and accommodation while D’Annunzio held Fiume and the reds gathered in the Po valley — that the Fascist historiography held up as the negative image of its own revolution.
