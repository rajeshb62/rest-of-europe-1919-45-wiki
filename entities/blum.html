Léon Blum

The Socialist in Power

Léon Blum, the leader of the French socialists, stood at the head of the Popular Front through its triumph and its agony — the first Socialist statesman to govern the Third Republic as president of the Council in 1936, restored to the same office in the ruinous spring of 1938, and the man who, in the closing months of the year, had to watch the movement he had made founder in the quarrels of its own parties. He had given the regime its governing experiment and its legend; the two great days of June 1936, the fausses grandes journées (the false great days) of the sit-down strikes and the social laws, had marked the French people in their usages and in their beings, as the conservative-liberal chronicler Daniel Halévy acknowledged even while deploring them. The front had shortened the working week, created the paid holiday, given the worker his two Sundays of rest and his August of deliverance — a mark on the habits of a nation that no later recovery of the state could wholly efface. But it had been made, Halévy insisted, by intellectuals jostled by demagogues, everything done against time and in the false; and Blum stood first among those who had “injected into the blood of the French people a drop of a very harmful morphine” from which it would be painful to be cured.

The Practice of Nothing

Halévy gave this chapter of drift and inaction the title la pratique du rien (the practice of nothing) — the art of governing by doing nothing practised by the parliamentary creators of delay, among whom the weary Chautemps was one of the best. By January 1938 the Popular Front was in its death-throes, and the chronicle of the year opens on the night of 13 January with the parliamentary scene of its collapse. Chautemps, president of the Council for seven months, was attempting the impossible task of limiting the damage of the Popular Front without breaking it, of succeeding Blum without pushing him aside. For Blum had retreated only a step: he had yielded the presidency of the Council, retaining the vice-presidency, always ready to know everything, to supervise everything and, if need be, to stop everything. Through the interminable night, as the Chamber drifted from suspension to suspension, it was Blum who held the soudure (the weld) of the two revolutionary parties together — the alliance of Socialists and Communists that he refused on any terms to see separated. When Chautemps finally broke with the Communists and the Socialists carried their resignations, Blum, summoned to the Palace-Bourbon “like the chief of an army compromised on the battlefield,” refused to admit the separation, and Chautemps, abandoned, left power.

The Statesman and the Crisis

The Austrian crisis exploded on Vienna in March, and it was then that Blum’s character was put to the test of the greatest public question. Hitler took Austria while Blum, in Halévy’s acid portrait, preoccupied himself with rendering presentable his “baroque Harlequin, crimson, red and pink” — a ministry in the image of the Popular Front, into which he sought to bring even a piece of the C.G.T. Behind all his combinations, Halévy detected a single dominant preoccupation: the accession of the Communists to government, that he might compromise these intimate and cruel rivals of his party by a share of responsibility. Of the national interest, the chronicler judged, there was no place in these calculations. And Blum, recalling his own famous self-definition — “I am not a Socialist man of state, I am a Socialist man of state,” the phrase he had coined at the tribune of the Senate in August 1936 — proved it once more. While the National deputies gathered, the veterans demanded a government of Public Safety, and the Chamber seethed with the sense that “Blum is keeping France waiting,” he remained closeted at the Quai Bourbon, conferring late with the Communist leaders Thorez and Duclos. When at last he proposed his National Rally to the National deputies, they listened in glacial silence, persuaded that he meant to make them accept a war dictatorship whose master would be his party, and rejected his demand by two hundred votes to three. Blum recalled the worn-out team of 1936 and took possession of the ministries.

The failure of the National Union attempt had a second political meaning. Blum’s party, once in office again, would never abandon the direction of the Front; and when the Chamber told him “Go away!” he remained, in a “lamentable persistence” that, Halévy judged, only heaped odium on his person and increased the discredit of France in Europe. The Senate waited to receive his first projects only to reject them and thus rid the country of his presence. When on 3 April 1938 Blum published his programme — an open inflation of ten milliards, a levy on fortunes, and the institution of multiple controls over the banks, the monopolised industries and the exchanges — he obtained in the Chamber no more than 310 votes against the 385 that had acclaimed him in June 1936. Wishing to retire, he was counselled by his own to go to the Senate and be beaten there, and the “socialist man of state” bowed. On 8 April the Senate, in the person of the old financier Caillaux — “the Senate is the assembly of the peasants; the Senate will reject your projects!” — rejected them, and the Blum government was done. His fall, which he had wished to make a resounding electoral declaration for the future, left the stage to Daladier.

The Era of Tyrannies

Blum’s fall in 1938 closed the last attempt of the Popular Front to govern. The movement had been a phenomenon of the era that the liberal historian Élie Halévy — the brother of the chronicler — diagnosed in his famous lecture L’ère des tyrannies (The Era of Tyrannies, 1936) as the age of the organised and totalitarian state, born of the convergence of total war and revolution since August 1914. The socialist movement, which Halévy had spent his life studying in England and France, had begun as the enemy of the state and ended, in the age of the war and the dictatorships, face to face with the question whether parliamentary democracy and socialism could be reconciled at all. Blum, the socialist who governed within the parliamentary republic while his Communist rivals pressed for the revolutionary road, incarnated that dilemma. In the closing weeks of 1938, after Munich, Blum adopted the idea of the indivisibility of peace and war — the Soviet idea — and the Socialists, meeting in congress on 25 December, divided over it; the chronicler left the year suspended, with the summations of the future gathering.

For Siegfried, writing in 1930 of the fan of parties, the socialists and radicals of the left had stood at the heart of the Third Republic’s unstable equilibrium, their balance struck between a heart on the left and a pocket on the right; for Zévaès, the narrative history of the republic closed in 1926 with the Cartel des Gauches in which the socialist and radical left had struggled against the same crushing problems as the Bloc National. Blum inherited that whole parliamentary tradition and carried it to its last and most desperate phase. His Socialist statesmanship, Halévy’s chronicle suggests, had been the final effort of the old parliamentary Left to govern a France it had itself demoralised; when it failed in April 1938, the way lay open to the restoration of authority that Daladier would attempt, and beyond that to the total war the era of tyrannies portended.
