Adolf Hitler

The Austrian Before the Age

Adolf Hitler was born at Braunau am Inn, on the Austrian side of the border, in the first half of 1889. The family name carried a dark tail — Schicklgruber — that the biographer Konrad Heiden, writing from exile in the third year of the Third Reich, turns over at length, though the man himself insisted on his true descent from the Hitler line of the Waldviertel. His father Alois, a customs official risen from the peasantry, wanted the boy to follow him into the civil service; the boy wanted to paint. He failed the examination of the Academy of Fine Arts in Vienna and sank, in his own telling, into years of poverty in the men’s hostels of the imperial capital, earning a meagre living as a draughtsman. Vienna taught him, he later wrote, the two lessons that became the axis of his thought: the bankruptcy of the multi-national Habsburg state, which he watched choke on its many peoples, and the “Jewish question,” which he claimed to have discovered in the streets, the press, and the working-class movement of the city. Heiden, more sceptical, notes that in those lost years Hitler drifted, attended by failure, without plan or connection — a “great inferior one” waiting for an age that would confer greatness on him.

The war of 1914 rescued him from aimlessness. Heiden calls it “the war as redeemer.” Hitler volunteered and served as a runner (Meldegänger) in a Bavarian regiment on the Western Front, was decorated with the Iron Cross, and found in the trenches the home he had never had. The collapse of November 1918 struck him, in his telling, at the hospital at Pasewalk, where he lay blinded by gas; there, he said, he resolved to take up politics and redeem the Germany betrayed by the November criminals. Heiden treats the Pasewalk revelation as myth rather than memory, but the vocation itself was real enough. After the war, posted in Munich as an instructor for returned soldiers, he attended a meeting of a small splinter group, the German Workers’ Party, and entered it as the seventh member — the moment at which, by his own account and Heiden’s, the movement begins.

The Orator and the Führerprinzip

What Hitler brought the tiny party was not doctrine but voice. In the beer halls of Munich he discovered that he could hold a crowd, and the crowd, not the programme, was his instrument. Heiden, who attended National Socialist meetings from 1922 and followed the movement from within, records the art: wit, logic and insolence, the hammering repetition of a few propositions, the listener made into a collaborator. “Hitler always says the same thing,” Heiden quotes, and the sameness was the point. He gathered around him the veterans and adventurers of postwar Munich — Dietrich Eckart, the poet who sensed a leader was needed; Ernst Röhm, the army captain of the “iron fist” who was already collecting weapons and Freikorps fighters; the young officers of the SA, the brown-shirted Sturmabteilung (storm detachment) that grew under his hand. The party adopted a flag and a symbol — the red banner with the Hakenkreuz (swastika) — and a programme of twenty-five points that Hitler himself would later quietly gut.

The organisation he imposed on the movement rested on the Führerprinzip (leadership principle): authority flows downward from an absolute chief, and every subordinate answers upward while being relieved of responsibility toward those below. Heiden offers the sharpest contemporary gloss on the idea. It meant, he writes, “for everyone relief from responsibility through an absolute supreme authority — security and moral comfort” — but it also meant that when responsibility downward was abolished, responsibility was abolished altogether, and the system of uncontrolled authority ended in despotism and the rule of favourites. Hitler’s own text supplies the doctrinal form: in Mein Kampf the state is the servant of the race, the parliamentary majority is rejected, and personality stands over the majority principle. The spoken word, he insists, is the force that sets the great historical avalanches rolling; propaganda is judged only by its success. Written in the fortress at Landsberg after the failure of the Beer Hall Putsch of November 1923 — the march on the Feldherrnhalle that collapsed under police fire — the book was at once a settlement of accounts with the age and a programme for seizing it.

The Seven Years on the Magic Mountain

The putsch of 1923 broke the movement, and Heiden traces Hitler’s long gamble on legitimacy through the years he calls “seven years on the magic mountain.” Hitler, tried before a sympathetic Munich court and imprisoned only briefly, decided that power was not to be stormed but awaited. The party was refounded; he withdrew to Haus Wachenfeld on the Obersalzberg and let the years do the work of the crisis. Two men rose beside him: Gregor Strasser, the Reich organisation leader who built the party’s machine in the north, and his young lieutenant Joseph Goebbels, whom Hitler, in 1926, made Gauleiter of Berlin, answerable to him alone. Inside the party, Hitler crushed the “Strasser-Goebbels front” that had plotted to unseat him, smashing the northern opposition at the Bamberg conference and binding the party to the unchanging twenty-five points under the absolute control of his Munich base. Outside, he courted money and respectability — the salons, the heavy industry, the conservative press — while Goebbels and Göring, returned from exile, fought the party’s internal battles for him.

The age repaid him. The crisis of 1929 and the collapse of the economy turned the small party into a mass one: from twelve seats in 1928 to a hundred and seven in 1930, then to the millions of the presidential year. Heiden’s account of the 1932 duel with Hindenburg is the pivot of the story. Hitler, who had once urged his followers to vote for the old field marshal, now ran against him, bidding the eighty-four-year-old president “go aside, old man.” Hindenburg won eighteen million votes to Hitler’s eleven in the first ballot, but the real victory was positional: the election made Hitler the recognised claimant to the state, and the men around the president — Schleicher, Papen, the East Prussian Junkers — began to bargain with him. In January 1933, after the fall of Schleicher and Gregor Strasser’s despairing resignation, the deal was struck. On 30 January, at noon, Hitler stood before Hindenburg and took the oath as Chancellor of the German Reich. Heiden notes the tears he shed when he came back to the Kaiserhof to Röhm, Goebbels and Göring.

The Break-In and the Absolute State

The seizure of power that followed was, in the official documentary record collected by Friedrichs, presented as a “national socialist revolution.” Hitler’s first steps combined legality and terror. The Reichstag fire of 27 February 1933 — which Heiden’s sources, in the Ernst document, lay at the door of Göring and Goebbels, who proposed the burning as a “Wahlbombe” and used the young Dutchman van der Lubbe as the decoy — produced the decree “for the protection of people and state” that suspended the guarantees of the constitution. The SA and SS were called in by Göring as auxiliary police, and the practical power of the state was taken into Nazi hands beyond the law. In the March elections the party won its plurality; in the first Reichstag of the Third Reich Hitler answered the Social Democrat Otto Wels and carried the Enabling Act, which delivered legislation into the hands of the government. The party state was dissolved; by July the NSDAP was the only legal party in Germany.

Hitler’s power was completed, as the regime’s own documents record, over the following year. The end of the party state, the Gleichschaltung (coordination) of the Länder under Reich governors, the rebuilding of economy and law under the new leadership — all flowed, in the official telling, from the Führer’s hand. The last obstacle to absolute rule was removed on 30 June 1934, in the action against the SA chief of staff Ernst Röhm, whom the regime charged with a treacherous plot. Heiden, writing a year later, gives a very different colour: an “unplanned dictatorship,” the purge of the SA leadership, the shooting of Schleicher and Gregor Strasser, Göring’s handling of the prince, the bloody settling of accounts across the Reich. The regime legalised the killings retroactively as Staatsnotwehr (state self-defence). Two months later, on the death of President von Hindenburg, Hitler united the offices of President and Chancellor in his own person and took the title Führer and Reichskanzler; the Wehrmacht swore its oath to him. Heiden closes his biography with the ashes of a Munich lawyer, shot on the evening of 30 June — “the ashes of the unknown German who fell victim to the highest responsibility assumed on 30 June.”
