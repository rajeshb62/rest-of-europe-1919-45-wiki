Paul von Hindenburg

The Father of the Field

By the time the Republic turned to him, Paul von Beneckendorff und von Hindenburg was already a monument. The East Prussian field marshal who had commanded the German armies through the First World War — the victor of Tannenberg, the figure whom the official eulogy of the regime would later call the “national myth of the German people” — had ended the war a shattered man, leading the armies home through the collapse of November 1918. The regime’s own commemorative text, published on his death in 1934, gives him a place in the founding of the Reich itself: as a young officer he had stood in the Hall of Mirrors at Versailles when the German Empire was proclaimed in 1871, and forty years later he was still “the quiet pole in the flight of appearances,” the symbol of German duty to which the nation clung when all else seemed to fall. Heiden, the exile biographer of Hitler, is more astringent, but the contemporary record is unanimous on one point: the old field marshal’s name was not a policy but a myth, and for that reason both the failing republic and its enemies sought to hold it.

After the war he retired, and seemed destined to end his days in the East Prussian quiet of Neudeck. But the republic was in search of a symbol. When President Ebert died in 1925, the parties of the Right — including, Heiden notes, the National Socialists, who had put up Ludendorff and seen him crushingly defeated — united on the old field marshal as their candidate. In the second ballot Hindenburg beat the Catholic leader Marx and the Communist Thälmann without commanding a majority of the whole electorate; under the rules of the constitution he was declared elected. Heiden remarks that this choice of a man of seventy-seven, who had spent his life in the army and had no experience of parliamentary government, was in reality the final installation of the Reichswehr at the centre of the state, for as President Hindenburg was also the supreme commander of the armed forces. The army, the “state within a state,” was now practically withdrawn from the interference of parliament and government. It was, Heiden concludes, a magnificent long-term speculation for Hitler, who had urged his followers to vote for the field marshal as the man in whom “the freedom-will of our people is paired with the rightness and uprightness of the greatest models of our history.”

The President and the Agitator

The presidency of Hindenburg spans the whole crisis of the Republic, and Heiden traces a widening gap between the old man and the political world around him. Surrounded by the East Prussian Junkers, the great landowners who bought him the estate of Neudeck and made his son Oskar its registered owner, the field marshal became the instrument of the agrarian and military interests that clustered at his ear. Heiden’s account of the “sham struggle against Hindenburg” in 1932 shows how the president’s authority became the terrain on which the last battles of the Republic were fought. Hitler, who had once courted him, now ran against him for the presidency. The campaign had a strange, almost chivalrous tone imposed by the challenger: Hitler publicly asked the old man to “fence chivalrously,” to treat the contest as not entirely serious, and then turned harsh — “Go aside, old man, for we do not wish to strike you, but those who hide behind you.” In the first ballot Hindenburg received eighteen million votes to Hitler’s eleven. The old man had won, but the duel itself had done the work: it made Hitler the acknowledged claimant to power and turned the field marshal’s entourage into a party of brokers.

Through 1932 the president disposed of chancellors and governments. Heiden’s narrative of these months is one of a state governed by the favour of the old man — the fall of Brüning, whom he dismissed at the urging of Schleicher and the Reichswehr; the appointment of the outsider Papen, whom the president “had taken a fancy to”; the shifting alliances of generals and landowners behind the scenes. On 13 August 1932, at the height of the crisis, Hitler came before the president to claim the chancellorship and was refused, sent down the stairs at the Palais by a man who distrusted the “Bohemian corporal.” Yet the very refusal was a step toward the transfer of power, for it bound the National Socialists into the game of cabinets and presidential confidence. The old field marshal remained, in Heiden’s phrase, “the Lord President,” the last authority of a disintegrating state, and every faction — the Reichswehr, the Centre, the Social Democrats, the Junkers, and finally Hitler himself — sought to stand under his protection.

The Opening of the Gates

On 30 January 1933 the culmination came. Hitler and Papen stood before the old man at noon; Papen reported that the national concentration had been achieved, and Hitler placed his hand in Hindenburg’s and swore his oath as Chancellor of the German Reich. The regime’s own documentary record gives the act its official meaning: on 30 January the president “opened the gates of the Reich for the young National Socialist movement,” and in trusting confidence handed responsibility to the hands of German youth, building “the bridge from yesterday to tomorrow.” Heiden’s account of the same scene is framed by the old man’s dwindling power — the joke of the entourage that Papen would rule in reality, the dream of taming the National Socialists from within. But Hindenburg’s role did not end with the appointment. His name remained the one independent authority left in the state, and his signature and person still lent the new government its constitutional cover.

The events of 1933 and 1934 unfolded under the presidency of the dying field marshal. The Reichstag fire, the Enabling Act, the end of the party state — all passed over his signature or under the shadow of his authority. In the summer of 1934, as the regime moved against the SA leadership, Hindenburg’s telegrams of 2 July thanked Hitler and confirmed the action, and the law of 3 July retroactively legalised the purge of 30 June as Staatsnotwehr (state self-defence). The old man was by then failing. His political testament, dated 11 May 1934 and delivered by von Papen on 15 August, addressed “the German people and their Chancellor,” recalling his 1919 legacy of duty and calling for the restoration of the old German spirit through the furnaces of suffering. On 2 August 1934 he died at Neudeck, at nine in the morning. The Reich government’s proclamation to the people announced that “Hindenburg is dead,” and Hitler, renouncing the title of president out of respect, took the offices of head of state and chancellor together as Führer and Reichskanzler. The Wehrmacht swore its oath to him; the field marshal was laid to rest with ceremony in the memorial at Tannenberg, his last service to the regime he had let into the Reich.
