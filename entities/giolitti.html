Giovanni Giolitti

The Old Liberal Statesman

Giovanni Giolitti, the old Piedmontese statesman who had dominated Italian politics before the war and governed again in the postwar crisis, appears in the Fascist sources as the representative of the liberalism that Fascism set out to destroy. The movement’s historians are unanimous in seeing in him not a tyrant but the emblem of the parliamentary system in its decadence. Farinacci, whose party history treats the Giolitti ministry at length, is the harshest: for the Cremona ras, Giolitti was the “dictator and corrupter of political struggle in Italy,” the master of manoeuvre who kept a decadent system alive by astuteness and opportunism. Pini and Businelli, more measured, present him as the skilled parliamentary manager who misread the age — the man who thought the old methods of compromise, pressure and the distribution of favours could still hold the state together against the rising tide of class war and nationalism.

The Return to Power

The sources date Giolitti’s return to the autumn of 1920, when the Nitti government collapsed and he was called back to the ministry he had held before the war. Pini’s chronicle marks the reappearance as the beginning of the resurgence — but a resurgence that Giolitti himself could not control. Farinacci’s second volume treats the “Giolitti al potere” at length, indicting his programme as peace at any cost and a policy of accommodation. It was Giolitti who confronted the red insurrection of 1920: the strikes and the occupation of the factories, which he neither suppressed by force nor allowed to succeed, preferring to let the wave break against its own impossibility. The sources are contemptuous of this passivity. Farinacci presents the occupation of the factories as a test that the government failed, the workers’ management collapsing from its own technical incapacity while Giolittian opportunism sacrificed the interests of all to the maintenance of a “demagogy of changed colour.” For the movement, the old statesman’s caution was the abdication of the state.

The Policy of Accommodation

Giolitti’s method, in the Fascist telling, was to hold the ring between the extremes and let them exhaust one another. The parecchio — the “good deal” or “something,” the phrase with which he was said to promise that Italy would get a good deal at the peace — became in the polemic a byword for his readiness to settle for less than the nation claimed. Against d’Annunzio’s Fiume he was patient, and finally ruthless: it was under Giolitti that the Treaty of Rapallo was signed with Yugoslavia in November 1920 and the Natale di sangue (Bloody Christmas) forced the poet’s legionaries out of the city. The sources do not forgive him the suppression of the enterprise that had seemed to embody the national will. At the same time he tolerated the rising Fascist squads, hoping to use them against the Socialists, and even brought them into the National Bloc of 1921. The Fascist historians hold this against him twice over: he both abandoned Fiume and miscalculated the force he had released.

The End of the Giolittian System

The elections of May 1921, fought within the National Bloc that Giolitti had constructed, returned thirty-five Fascists to the Chamber and marked the point at which the movement ceased to be his instrument. The sources see the dissolution of the Chamber and the electoral struggle as Giolitti’s last manoeuvre — Farinacci calls the dissolution the “extreme attempt,” the “perfect field of manoeuvre,” the work of seduction — and its result as the failure of the old politics. Within weeks Giolitti abandoned power, and the ministries that followed, of Bonomi and Facta, proved unable to govern. The third volume of Farinacci’s history names this whole collapse La fine del sistema giolittiano — the end of the Giolittian system — and presents the March on Rome of October 1922 as the settlement of the account opened by his return. The old statesman’s liberalism, in the movement’s verdict, had been shown to be a system of palliatives that neither defended the nation nor governed it, and Fascism came as its successor and its negation.
